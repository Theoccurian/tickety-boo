Potatoes can thrive in imitation moon soil just as well as they do in ordinary Earth soil, provided it is mixed with enough organic matter. Researchers at Oregon State University found that the plants completed their entire life cycle in the simulated lunar material, and that the resulting potatoes were safe to eat.

The moon's surface is blanketed in regolith, a layer of broken rock and dust that holds very little organic matter. To see whether a crop could cope, the team used regolith simulants and blended in varying amounts of vermicompost, a rich soil booster made by letting earthworms break down organic waste. The findings appear in the journal npj Microgravity.

The proportion made all the difference. Plants in simulants containing 30% vermicompost kept pace with those in Earth soils, both in how they grew and how they developed. With no compost, they were badly stunted and had poorly formed roots, while 5% to 15% closed the gap only in part.

The lunar surface is far from uniform, so the researchers used five different simulants, standing in for both the dark mare plains and the highland regions. As a yardstick, they also grew potatoes in soil from Hermiston, Oregon, an area with a reputation for exceptionally productive potato farming.

Regolith also tends to contain raised levels of heavy metals, which can damage roots and slow growth. The team believes organic matter binds these metals and so limits how much the plants take in. David Handy, a postdoctoral researcher in the lab, said the compost seems to offset some of the toxicity by reducing exposure to harmful compounds.

Crucially, the potatoes did not lose their nutritional value. There was also no statistically significant increase in glycoalkaloids, the naturally occurring potato toxins often linked to green patches beneath the skin.

Potatoes were picked because they deliver plenty of energy from a small patch of ground, are highly nutritious and are regarded as a practical staple for lengthy space missions. The work continues, with a forthcoming paper set to report on 10 potato varieties grown in the simulants, to find which suit lunar farming best.

## Why it matters

NASA's Artemis programme aims to establish a lasting human presence on the moon, and that will require growing food there rather than relying on costly resupply from Earth. This research suggests astronauts could carry a small stock of vermicompost and then make more from food scraps and plant waste. It is a cracking early step, though the experiments used simulated soil rather than genuine lunar material.
