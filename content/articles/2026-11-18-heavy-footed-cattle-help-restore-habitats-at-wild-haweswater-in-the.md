A herd of 53 Belted Galloway and Luing cattle is quietly doing valuable conservation work in the Lake District, and the first snow of the season suits them nicely. Bred in Scotland, the animals have grazed the RSPB's Wild Haweswater site for some years as part of a conservation grazing project.

The aim is to mimic the wild plant-eaters that lived in the area before the bronze age. Site manager Glen Swainson explains that the cattle are big and heavy, and their tread flattens the denser, more dominant plants. That gives delicate species and young trees space to get started.

Trees are becoming ever more important here. They slow the movement of water through the landscape, which lowers the risk of floods and droughts, and they offer shelter to livestock in Swindale.

The breeds are well suited to the fells. Mr Swainson says short legs keep them close to the ground, while a thick double coat and broad back help them cope with rough terrain and harsh weather. Their tongues are useful too, as they curl around plants and pull. That leaves vegetation at varied heights, which benefits insects, small mammals, amphibians, reptiles and birds.

In his view, the native wildlife and plants of Haweswater developed alongside this sort of grazing and trampling. Traditional breeds therefore have plenty to offer when degraded habitats need restoring.

Winter is planned for, too. In late summer, the Swindale wildflower meadows are cut so that a rich variety of wildflowers and grasses can set seed. That material is then used as hay for the cattle, and for Cumbrian fell ponies that also trample the ground, when the weather is at its worst. It is laid on patches dominated by rushes and bracken, so the animals tread them down as they eat.

The herd is often divided into smaller groups and moved regularly, so that no single patch is overgrazed.

## Why it matters

Restoring degraded upland habitats is no mean feat, and grazing animals that suit the landscape offer a practical, low-tech way to go about it. Similar schemes in southern Scotland and Berkshire use cattle to restore rare heathland and boost biodiversity. More trees and richer plant life in the hills can mean better homes for wildlife and, downstream, a little more protection against floods and droughts.
