Antarctic blue whales have staged a remarkable comeback, rising from an estimated 418 animals in 1973 to 4,018 today. That amounts to average growth of 4.5% a year since the harpoons were laid down for good.

The International Whaling Commission banned the killing of these whales in 1964, yet illegal hunting carried on until 1973. Recovery was never going to be speedy. Blue whales live a long time, the biggest creatures known ever to have existed, and a female produces just one calf roughly every three years.

Working out how many there are was no mean feat. The Southern Ocean population spends most of its time below the surface, well out of sight of anyone hoping to count heads.

Zoe Rand, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Washington, led the study. Her approach was to pull together three different strands of evidence: records of whales caught in the whaling era, photographs that allow individual animals to be recognised today, and results from decades of "line transect" surveys.

Those surveys took place in three phases between 1978 and 2004. Vessels sailed along straight lines across the Southern Ocean while observers logged each whale they spotted and how far it was from the boat. Taken together, the evidence showed not only that numbers were rising, but that they had risen a great deal.

None of this means the story is finished. The IUCN still classes blue whales as critically endangered, and the Antarctic population remains a fraction of its size before whaling began. Even so, an animal making this sort of progress would probably not carry that label for much longer.

## Why it matters

The finding shows that a species driven to the brink can recover once the killing stops, even when it breeds as slowly as blue whales do. Rand has described whales as ecosystem engineers: by feeding, defecating and eventually dying, they shape huge stretches of ocean, and thousands of other creatures respond to what they do. A growing blue whale population is therefore rather good news for the wider marine environment, not only for the whales themselves.
