Each generation seems to arrive in old age with sharper thinking skills than the one before, and to lose them at a gentler pace. That is the finding of a large study in Nature Medicine, and it suggests the point at which decline becomes serious enough to count as dementia is arriving later.

The work was led by Anders Fjell, who directs the LCBC research centre, a group that studies how the brain and cognition change over a lifetime. His team analysed data on about 219,000 people who were tracked for up to nearly 30 years.

Younger generations have long been known to do better in cognitive tests. What the researchers add is that the ageing process itself looks as though it has moved further into the future. By their estimate, the gaps are large enough to explain the lower dementia rates recorded in Europe and the US, put at around 13% per decade.

Following the same individuals for so long was essential, according to Fjell. A snapshot of different age groups cannot tell you whether they differ because of ageing or because they began from different starting points. Repeated testing over many years can separate the two.

The study cannot pin down a single cause. Fjell suggests the great social changes of the 20th century are the likeliest candidates: longer schooling, better food, improved conditions for children and teenagers, and better health care. Newer heart medicines and cleaner air may have played a part too. "It is probably not a single cause," he says.

There is a caution attached. Fjell stresses that these gains were built by deliberate improvements and did not happen by themselves. Trends in physical activity, overweight and inequality among today's young people do not necessarily point in the right direction.

The research compares generations, not individuals, so the team offers no personal advice. Its next job is to work out who has gained most, and whether better living conditions protect people equally whatever their genetic vulnerability.

## Why it matters

Dementia touches a great many families, so evidence that the risk can fall from one generation to the next is rather good news. It suggests that risk is shaped by conditions society can influence, from maternal health and childhood nutrition to schooling and heart health. That points towards prevention that begins early in life and is treated as a shared public task, not simply a matter of personal responsibility.
