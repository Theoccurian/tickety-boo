Small creatures are making a comeback on two remote sub-Antarctic islands once overrun by invasive rodents. Fresh research shows that invertebrates on Macquarie and Antipodes islands are now more plentiful, and more species are turning up across the survey sites, than before the pests were removed.

By 2014, Macquarie Island had been cleared of rats, mice and rabbits. Antipodes Island has been mouse-free since 2018. Getting there was no mean feat: helicopters scattered poison bait, GPS guided the drops, and detection dogs followed up to check that no survivors remained.

To see what happened next, the team set traps for surface-crawling invertebrates at a range of sites over several years, revisiting the same spots each time. One expert then spent hundreds of hours at the microscope identifying what had been caught. The results were compared with older records the researchers hunted down, covering 30 years on Macquarie and eight on Antipodes.

Spiders and fly larvae, both of which mice had preyed upon, bounced back especially strongly. On Antipodes, native spiders, beetles, worms and pseudoscorpions made the biggest gains, while on Macquarie it was native flies that fared best.

The researchers think the contrast is down to the islands' histories. Rabbits grazed Macquarie's grasslands and herbfields for more than 150 years, and over a decade after they were removed the vegetation has still not fully regrown. That means less habitat for invertebrates to return to. Macquarie had rats as well as mice, too.

There is a sobering note. An undescribed species of cave weta on Antipodes is thought to have been lost to mice. Even so, the wider picture is rather good news for the islands' smaller residents.

The authors add that Lord Howe Island, off Australia, has been free of rats and mice since 2019, and that its native invertebrates and birds are recovering as well.

## Why it matters

Islands are home to many species found nowhere else, and they have suffered a heavy share of the world's extinctions, much of it at the paws of introduced predators. Seabirds are known to recover once rodents go, but insects, spiders and worms, which help break down organic matter and recycle nutrients, receive far less attention. This work shows they benefit too, strengthening the case for clearing more islands. It also shows the job is never quite finished: careful biosecurity for visitors and cargo is needed to keep rodents from returning.
