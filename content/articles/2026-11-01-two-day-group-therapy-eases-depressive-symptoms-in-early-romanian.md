A two-day course of group therapy that invites people to view emotional pain through an evolutionary lens has eased depressive symptoms in a small early trial in Romania. A week after treatment, those who took part were doing markedly better than adults who were still waiting for their turn.

The method, called cognitive evolutionary therapy, builds on cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), which looks at how thoughts, feelings and actions feed one another. It adds a further question: how do struggles with belonging, close relationships and safety tie in with needs that mattered to our ancestors? The aim is to soften the self-blame that can settle on top of low mood, for instance after a break-up.

The team randomly placed 61 adults with depressive symptoms into two groups. Thirty-one received the therapy and 30 joined a waiting list. Treatment was packed into two back-to-back eight-hour days in Bucharest, with five to seven people in each group. That adds up to 16 hours, comparable to what someone might get from 12 to 16 individual CBT appointments.

Participants filled in questionnaires about difficulties in their lives beforehand. In the sessions, the therapist worked with one person at a time while the rest looked on, and a guided round of feedback and discussion followed. No homework was set in between.

On a depression questionnaire scored from 0 to 42, the therapy group was about 7.5 points better off than the waiting list at one week, which counts as a large statistical effect. Anxiety and life satisfaction improved too.

By three months, the gap had narrowed to roughly 3.1 points, which cannot be called statistically significant. People on the waiting list had also improved over that time, and the researchers say the small sample leaves them unable to say whether a meaningful benefit lasted.

They are open about other limits. Six people assigned to therapy never turned up, one therapist delivered all of the treatment, and a co-author developed the therapy. Results rested on self-reporting, and a formal diagnosis was not required to take part.

## Why it matters

Depression is common, and a standard course of individual therapy can mean many weeks of appointments, so a far shorter option would be welcome if it proves its worth. This trial is only a first step, and the researchers call for larger, independent studies that compare the programme with standard group CBT and follow people for longer. Even so, it is rather encouraging that a brief, carefully structured approach produced a clear early benefit.
