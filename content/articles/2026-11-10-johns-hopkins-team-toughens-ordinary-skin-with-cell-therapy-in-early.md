Researchers at Johns Hopkins Medicine say they have partly transformed ordinary human skin into volar skin, the tough, pressure-resistant kind found only on the palms and soles. The work comes from a phase 1 clinical trial, published on 10 November in the Journal of Investigative Dermatology.

The aim is to help people who use prosthetic limbs. An estimated 2 million people in the US live with limb loss, and bruising and irritation where a prosthesis attaches can limit how much they can use it. Volar skin is naturally hardy, but limb loss often removes it, so finding enough tissue to use in treatment is a real challenge.

Ten healthy adult volunteers, with an average age of 36.5, took part in the five-month study. Each gave cells called fibroblasts from the scalp and from the sole of the foot, which were grown and multiplied in the laboratory. At separate sites, every volunteer then received control injections, scalp-cell injections and sole-cell injections.

The team also tried something new. Before the cell therapy, a single injection of a cosmetic filler containing collagen and PMMA, a synthetic polymer, was placed at the chosen sites. Study lead Sam S. Lee explained that "Skin fibroblasts naturally attach to collagen", hence the idea of giving them something to cling to.

At two months and five months, the researchers measured skin thickness, firmness and changes in the cells. As expected, skin treated with sole fibroblasts became more like volar skin, with greater thickness and elasticity. The filler neither stopped these changes nor improved them dramatically.

There was one surprise. The filler drew the volunteers' own fibroblasts, blood vessel cells and immune cells to every treatment site, a sign of an immune response. This did not alter how well the treatment worked over the five months. Lee suspects the PMMA is responsible, as the body cannot break it down, and longer studies will be needed to see what the response means over time.

Next, the team plans to test whether growth factors or physical stimulation could strengthen or stabilise the volar-like qualities.

## Why it matters

Skin breakdown at the point where a prosthesis meets the body is a familiar problem, and growing tougher skin there could one day make artificial limbs more comfortable to wear. This is early, small-scale work in healthy volunteers rather than amputees, and the lasting effects are still unknown. Even so, it is a welcome step towards a treatment that borrows the body's own toughest skin.
