Glowworms are being coaxed back to a North Yorkshire nature reserve where they have not been seen for 100 years, thanks to a volunteer with a garden shed and a great deal of patience.

Peter Cooper, a local wildlife enthusiast, collects and breeds the insects in his spare time. He then releases their larvae at Nosterfield Nature Reserve. This year he has 1,900 larvae in the shed, due to be set free there.

The reserve's manager, Simon Warwick, credited Cooper's "raw passion and enthusiasm" for bringing the glow back after so long. Glowworms are incredibly rare in the region, he said, and the volunteers have been thrilled by the project.

Nosterfield is described as one of North Yorkshire's most important wetland wildlife sites. It covers around 600 acres of old limestone quarries. Some of the land was reclaimed naturally, while the rest was rewilded by the nonprofit Lower Ure Conservation Trust. The result is a habitat well suited to the Lampyridae family, which includes fireflies, lightning bugs and glowworms.

The poet Edmund Bogg once likened their lights to lamps lit by fairy folk. Glowworms were once abundant across Britain, but they have since gone dark across wide stretches of the island, and bringing them back is notoriously difficult.

Cooper and fellow volunteers, who call themselves "glow-getters", have slowly built up expertise in rearing the larvae in captivity. It is delicate work, and a plucky bit of home-grown conservation.

There is still a wait ahead. Cooper says the project cannot be judged a success until the wild-bred offspring of his shed-reared larvae reach maturity and light up the Yorkshire darkness themselves. That is not expected until sometime in 2030.

## Why it matters

Once a species has vanished from an area, it can be very hard to coax back, and glowworms are among those that struggle to re-establish. This project shows how restored habitat and committed local volunteers can work together to give a lost species another chance. The long wait for results is a reminder that conservation often runs on patient, small-scale effort rather than quick wins.
