A male rufous hummingbird has completed the longest migration ever recorded for its kind, covering 6,526 kilometres (4,055 miles) between Alabama and Alaska over 38 days. The bird is roughly sparrow-sized and no heavier than a sugar cube, which makes it no mean feat.

Its progress was followed with a miniature tag developed by animal physiologist Emma Rhodes, of the University of Memphis, and her team. Their field tests appear in the journal Ecology and Evolution.

Hummingbirds have always been awkward to monitor. Most weigh under 5 grams, so the trackers used on bigger migrants are far too heavy. This new device weighs only 0.06 grams, about the same as a grain of rice.

It has no battery and no memory. A small solar cell powers a transmitter that sends out a signal every 3 seconds, and a smartphone or computer within 200 metres can pick it up over Bluetooth.

In March and April 2025, the researchers fitted tags to four rufous and seven ruby-throated hummingbirds as they began their spring journeys from coastal Alabama and the Florida Panhandle. Each tag stayed below 3 per cent of the bird's weight and was glued between the shoulder blades.

Because the routes could be watched almost as they happened, some unexpected detours came to light. One rufous drifted along a winding path through several eastern states for 23 days, including a loop of about 345 kilometres into eastern Tennessee. Two ruby-throated males banded on the same day headed north in near-perfect parallel for two days, about 83 kilometres apart. The record-setting rufous went west across northern Mexico and then straight up the Pacific Flyway.

The tags have their limits. They cannot transmit at night, and they are of little use where there are no phones or computers within range. They also lasted an average of almost 47 days before the glue presumably failed.

## Why it matters

Bird bands have taught researchers a good deal about where hummingbirds travel, but populations are declining and scientists need finer detail to understand where and why. A tag that ordinary phones can detect could turn a great many pockets into a ready-made network of receivers. Rhodes and her colleagues note that both rufous and ruby-throated hummingbirds have recently fallen in number, and they expect the technology to inform conservation and species management.
