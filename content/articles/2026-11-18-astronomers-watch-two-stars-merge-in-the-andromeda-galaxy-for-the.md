Astronomers have caught two stars merging in the Andromeda galaxy while it was actually happening, a first of its kind. A star slightly lighter than the Sun spiralled into a bloated supergiant on the galaxy's outskirts, leaving a single star that the team has nicknamed Bao.

The NASA-funded discovery is reported in the journal Nature. Rapid follow-up from the James Webb Space Telescope, cross-checked against data from other telescopes in space and on the ground, backed it up. Webb looked just 47 days after the eruption peaked, the earliest follow-up of an event like this.

For millennia the pair had orbited one another quietly. Once the smaller star touched the giant's surface, the union took only seven orbits, or about seven years. Other known mergers of binary stars, involving smaller stars, took thousands of orbits.

The team's modelling suggests the larger star, dubbed "Big Bao", had seven times the Sun's mass, 350 times its radius and shone 18,000 times brighter. Its companion, "Little Bao", weighed in at about 0.7 solar masses. The numbers first hinted that the smaller star might escape, but further analysis showed it was dragged into the giant's core.

The trail began in February 2023, when researchers noticed an unusual variable star in data from NASA's NEOWISE mission. By September 2024 it was brightening and shedding dust. The Zwicky Transient Facility detected the eruption on 17 October 2025, and Webb's observations followed on 24 December 2025. Piecing that sequence together as it unfolded is no mean feat.

What Bao looks like now remains to be seen. Only future observations will show how the new star settles down.

The study's first author, Viraj Karambelkar of Columbia University, expects plenty more discoveries of this sort soon, with NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope about to begin large public surveys.

## Why it matters

Many binary stars pass through a messy stage in which they share an envelope of gas. Some pairs survive it and go on to become neutron stars, black holes or sources of gravitational waves. Astronomers have usually had to work backwards from events that happened long ago, so watching one unfold lets them measure directly how the process works. It also gives them a method they can reuse on the similar events expected to turn up in future surveys.
