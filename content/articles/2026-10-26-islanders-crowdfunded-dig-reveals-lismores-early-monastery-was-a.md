Residents of Lismore, a small island in Scotland's Inner Hebrides, have helped to show that the early medieval monastery on their doorstep was a busy hub of worship, skilled craftwork and learning. Researchers now think its standing may have matched that of some of the most celebrated monastic sites in Britain at the time.

The Lismore Historical Society commissioned the excavation, and a sizeable share of the island's 170 residents chipped in to pay for it. Dr Clare Ellis of Argyll Archaeology led the work around the site of the island's 13th-century cathedral.

The monastery, called Lios Mòr, was established by Saint Mo Luóc in the 6th century. Until now, its story rested on written records, place-names and local tradition, with precious little physical proof of its size or character. The team wanted to find out whether the cathedral site also held Mo Luóc's original monastery, and how large and important the settlement had been.

Excavation and geophysical survey revealed extensive burial grounds, early buildings, high-status craftworking areas and rare signs of precious-metal working. In one spot, six burials dated between 650 and 1040 CE lay among fragments of large carved stone crosses. Elsewhere, an oval building on a limestone-paved base was surrounded by traces of handmade pottery and toolmaking.

A third trench produced around 1,000 fragments of debris linked to precious metalwork. It also held small crucibles, probably used to melt copper, silver and gold before pouring it into casts, and some of the casts themselves.

Writing in the journal Antiquity, the researchers suggest this production most likely took place inside the monastic enclosure. Items such as brooches hint that secular people may have had an influence or presence there. They add that monasteries in Ireland at around the same time made secular goods for trade, which points to economic links between elite lay and church centres.

Professor Gordon Noble of the University of Aberdeen, a co-author of the paper, said the monastery could now be seen as a flourishing centre rather than a name in old documents. Bob Hay, a former islander who organised the funding and volunteers, said the project showed what community-led archaeology can add to academic research.

For a plucky little historical society, it is a cracking result.

## Why it matters

Written records and local tradition can only take history so far, and physical evidence is what turns a good story into established knowledge. By showing that Lismore was a major centre of faith and craft, the dig adds to our picture of how Christianity spread and flourished in early medieval Britain. It also shows that a small community, with modest funds and willing volunteers, can make a genuine contribution to scholarship.
