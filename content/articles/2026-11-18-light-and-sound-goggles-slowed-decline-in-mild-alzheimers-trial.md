People with mild Alzheimer's disease who wore a device delivering flickering light and sound at 40Hz declined more slowly than untreated volunteers, according to results from a large national trial. The findings were announced in Boston at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease conference by Cognito Therapeutics, a company spun out of MIT.

The "HOPE" trial involved 663 people, split into a mild group of 395 and a moderate group of 268. It was randomised, controlled and double-blind, and it ran for 12 months. In the mild group, the company reported slowed decline of 56% on one measure of daily function (ADCS-ADL), 53% on another (ADCS-iADL) and 47% on a standard rating of dementia severity (CDR-SB).

Results were stronger among 169 mild-stage volunteers who used their devices for at least 70% of the intended time. Their decline slowed by 82% on both daily-function measures and by 64% on the severity rating. A fourth measure, iADMARS, also reached statistical significance, at 73%. Volunteers tolerated the goggles well, with no unanticipated adverse device effects observed.

The picture was not uniformly rosy. The moderate group showed no clinical benefit, mild patients did not reach statistical significance on some measures, and the study as a whole missed its primary endpoint. Cognito's chief executive, Christian Howell, acknowledged that the company had hoped for an effect across the whole population and did not see one.

Even so, the company says it will work with the US Food and Drug Administration towards authorisation of the device, called Spectris, for mild Alzheimer's. The approach traces back to work begun at MIT's Picower Institute in 2014. Professor Li-Huei Tsai, who co-founded the company, called the result "a dream come true".

Earlier human studies had pointed the same way. A 2022 pilot with 15 mild-stage volunteers reported preserved hippocampus volume after three months, and Cognito's 76-person OVERTURE trial found no serious adverse results. Tsai now plans a study to see whether the approach could prevent symptoms in people at risk.

## Why it matters

Alzheimer's treatments that are noninvasive and well tolerated would be a welcome addition for patients and families who have few options, particularly early in the disease when preserving independence matters most. These are company-reported results from a trial that missed its main goal overall, so regulators will need to weigh them carefully. Still, a clear signal in the mild group is a cautiously encouraging step.
