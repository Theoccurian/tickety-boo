Physicists have coaxed X-rays out of helium atoms at energies that standard laser theory says should barely be possible. The secret is persuading two electrons to give up their energy at precisely the same instant. The results, from a team at TU Wien and the University of California San Diego, are published in Nature Photonics.

For decades, researchers have known that firing laser light at certain atoms makes them emit very brief flashes of higher-frequency light, X-rays among them. In the textbook account, the laser pulls a single electron free, sends it away from its atom and then drives it back. The energy it sheds on returning leaves as light.

That account comes with a ceiling. Theory fixes a maximum energy, called the cutoff, according to the properties of the incoming laser. Beyond it, the X-ray signal fades to almost nothing.

Tenio Popmintchev's group at the Institute of Photonics in Vienna wanted to know what would happen if two electrons joined in. They aimed intense ultraviolet laser pulses at helium and arranged for both of its electrons to be removed, one after the other. Although the pair broke free at different moments, quantum correlations kept their behaviour linked.

By shaping the pulses with great care, the team made both electrons arrive back at the atom together. Their combined energy was then released in a single burst, which can produce one X-ray photon of higher frequency than the old limit permits. That is no mean feat.

The proof lay in the spectrum of the light emitted. A second plateau of steady emission showed up at far higher photon energies than the standard picture predicts. It was fainter than the main plateau, but it stretched well past the familiar range.

Helium was the only gas to show it. Comparable runs with argon and neon, looking at their outer electrons, produced no such effect. Helium's two electrons are especially strongly correlated, which fits the explanation the researchers put forward.

The team believes this unusual emission could serve as a sensitive probe of how electrons influence one another over attoseconds, each of which is a billionth of a billionth of a second. Later on, the approach could be tried on molecules and solid materials where electrons interact strongly.

There is a pleasing sense of continuity here. Laser methods developed at the same Vienna institute in the 1990s contributed to work honoured with the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physics, and the technique involved, high-harmonic generation, helped create attosecond science in the first place.

## Why it matters

Electrons that affect one another strongly sit at the heart of many quantum systems and advanced materials, yet they move so fast that they are very hard to study. A fresh, sensitive way of reading their correlations could help research into quantum computing and the design of nanomaterials with carefully controlled properties. It is also a cracking reminder that even well-established rules of physics can hold surprises when someone tries a different set-up.
