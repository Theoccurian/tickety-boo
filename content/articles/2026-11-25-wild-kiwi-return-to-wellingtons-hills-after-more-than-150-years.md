Wild kiwi are living in the hills around Wellington for the first time in more than 150 years. Over 250 birds have been moved to the area since late 2022, and once chicks hatched in the wild are counted, the local population is thought to exceed 300.

The effort is run by Capital Kiwi, a nonprofit led by iwi (Māori nations), community members and private landowners working together. Its founder and lead, Paul Ward, says the aim was to bring these flightless birds, known as manu in te reo Māori, back to the capital's hills.

Preparing the ground was no mean feat. Stoats, which Europeans introduced in the late 1800s to control rabbits, are the main danger to young kiwi. Before any birds were released, teams laid out upwards of 5,300 traps over a 24,000-hectare patchwork of working farmland, forestry and suburban reserves.

Ward says that where predators are not controlled, nearly all chicks die before reaching adulthood. Under this project's trapping regime, more than 90% of chicks have survived. He puts the "extraordinary result" down to the release areas being rich in food, so that, with predators managed, adult birds are well able to fend for themselves.

Rawiri Walsh, who serves as the project's kiwi guardian and as iwi liaison for Taranaki Whānui, says monitoring shows the released birds are in good condition. They are also laying eggs.

Each translocation follows traditional Māori protocols. Iwi that give up birds do so in answer to a formal gifting request, called a tono, and the communities receiving them hold a welcoming ceremony, or pōwhiri. Walsh sees releasing a kiwi into its burrow as putting something back into the land. "We believe that we're an integral part of that whenua," he says.

Looking ahead, the team plans to lean on its relationships with iwi, landowners and communities. Ward says they want to explore which other treasured species, or taonga, might be returned to these places.

## Why it matters

Kiwi are among New Zealand's most cherished birds, and introduced predators such as stoats have long threatened their young. Here, high chick survival and signs of breeding suggest the birds are settling in, a cracking result for a species absent from these hills for well over a century. The project also shows iwi, landowners and residents working side by side, with traditional protocols guiding each release.
