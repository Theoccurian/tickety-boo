On 27 October 1962, at the height of the Cuban Missile Crisis, a Soviet officer named Vasily Arkhipov held out against a decision that could have ended in nuclear war. His refusal to go along with it is why he is remembered today as a man who may have spared the world a catastrophe.

That day, American warships, eleven destroyers plus the carrier USS Randolph, tracked down the Soviet submarine B-59 near Cuba. The diesel-powered boat carried nuclear weapons. Though it lay in international waters, the Americans began dropping depth charges as a signal, hoping to compel it to come up and identify itself.

Aboard the submarine, the mood must have been tense. The crew had heard nothing from Moscow, and no civilian radio broadcasts, for days, so they had no way of knowing whether war had already broken out. Captain Valentin Grigoryevich Savitsky reasoned that it might have, and he wanted to fire a nuclear torpedo.

On Soviet submarines carrying this weapon, only the captain and the political officer needed to approve a launch. B-59, however, also had Arkhipov aboard as Chief of Staff of the submarine flotilla, so three agreeing votes were required instead of two.

A fierce argument broke out among the three men, with Arkhipov alone opposed. In time he talked Savitsky into surfacing and waiting for instructions from Moscow, which was no mean feat given the pressure they were under.

Recognition took a long while to arrive. Most of the crew were held in low regard back home, although Arkhipov stayed in the Navy and eventually became a Rear Admiral. In a 2012 PBS documentary, his wife Olga remembered him as clever, courteous and unruffled.

In 2002, Russian officials held a press conference and revealed that B-59 had been carrying nuclear torpedoes, and that Arkhipov was the reason they stayed unused. In 2017, his family received the first Future of Life Award on his behalf, presented after his death.

## Why it matters

The Cuban Missile Crisis is widely seen as one of the most perilous moments of the Cold War, and this episode shows how much can hinge on one person's judgement when information is scarce and nerves are frayed. Arkhipov's decision to hold back and wait for word from Moscow avoided a launch that would probably have led to a nuclear conflict. It is a reminder that restraint under pressure can matter as much as any show of force.
