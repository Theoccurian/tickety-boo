Scientists at UC Davis Health have explained how an experimental antibody can reverse type 1 diabetes in early laboratory studies. Their findings, published in the journal Diabetes, could help guide the search for treatments for people newly diagnosed with the condition.

Type 1 diabetes arises when the immune system wrongly attacks the beta cells in the pancreas that make insulin. As those cells are lost, the body can no longer keep blood sugar in check, and patients need insulin for life. Current treatments manage the disease but do not halt the underlying autoimmune attack.

Most experimental therapies target T cells, part of the body's adaptive immune system. The UC Davis team, led by William Ridgway, looked instead at the innate immune system, a far less-studied part of the picture. Using advanced single-cell sequencing, they found the antibody prompted the production of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, specialised immune cells that help keep inflammation in check. These cells travelled to the pancreatic islet and dampened the immune activity that damages beta cells.

The treatment also relied on interleukin-10, an anti-inflammatory signalling molecule. Without it, the suppressor cells did not develop and the therapy lost its power to reverse the disease.

Perhaps the most striking result concerned the beta cells themselves. Soon after treatment, markers of cellular stress fell sharply, including pathways known as endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response, both linked to beta-cell damage. Ridgway described "the profound reduction in beta-cell stress" as a particular highlight, and said it appears to be the first close look at the pancreas after the disease has been reversed.

The team believes easing the strain on surviving insulin-producing cells may be an important part of restoring healthy blood sugar levels.

It is worth being clear about the stage of the work. This is early laboratory research, and more is needed before any studies in people can begin. Future work will look at adapting the approach for human therapies and whether similar mechanisms can be safely triggered in patients.

## Why it matters

Most people are diagnosed with type 1 diabetes once significant damage to their beta cells has already been done, so treatments that can reverse the disease, rather than merely prevent it, are what patients really need. By identifying the immune cells, the signalling molecule and the drop in beta-cell stress involved, this research gives scientists specific biological pathways to aim at. The researchers also suggest the insights could eventually inform work on other autoimmune diseases, though that remains a hope rather than a result.
