A wild population that has shrunk to a few dozen animals is not necessarily heading for oblivion, according to a major new study. Researchers from the Zoological Society of London and Monash University found that well-aimed conservation work can haul birds and mammals back from the very edge. The results appear in Proceedings of the Royal Society B.

The paper challenges a long-standing theory that groups squeezed this small are bound to fail, undone by inbreeding and a dwindling pool of genetic diversity. To test it, the team looked at birds and mammals that had dropped beneath 50 animals in the wild at any point since 1950.

Of those, 141 species had records detailed enough to analyse. Some 51 of them were given direct, practical help, including control of invasive predators, restoration of habitat, breeding in captivity and moves to new locations. Among that group, 61% grew to at least 100 individuals.

Species left without such support fared far worse. Some 37% of them were lost to extinction, while only 2% reached a population of 100 or more.

The strongest results came when conservationists combined action against the threats behind a decline with direct management of the animals themselves. Australia's Lord Howe woodhen is a cracking example. It numbered just 23 birds in the late 1970s and today stands at more than 1,600.

Lead author Dr Donal Smith, of Monash University's School of Biological Sciences, called the findings an empirically grounded note of hope for global biodiversity. He said that a population's size alone should not decide whether a species is given up on. When people tackle the outside pressures and support numbers directly, he said, small populations can recover remarkably well.

His colleague Rohan Clarke, also of the Monash School of Biological Sciences, made the case against writing off the most precarious species through hard-nosed triage. He accepted that genetic risks are real, but said the track record shows that human intervention works, and that backing science-led management is far from futile.

## Why it matters

Conservation money is finite, and decisions about where to spend it can leave the rarest species at the back of the queue if they are judged beyond saving. This study offers rather good news, because it suggests that a tiny population is not a lost cause in itself. With the right mix of protection from threats and direct support, species down to their last few dozen individuals can still have a future.
