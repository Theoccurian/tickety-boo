Conservationists have a new step-by-step guide for showing whether work in one place is helping to stop species disappearing worldwide. The framework, published in the journal Conservation Biology, comes from Newcastle University researchers working with IUCN, the International Union for Conservation of Nature.

It builds on a tool called STAR, short for Species Threat Abatement and Restoration. STAR estimates how much the risk of extinction could fall if threats were reduced, or habitat restored, in a particular area. The new paper concentrates on the threat-reduction half of the tool, known as START.

The process begins with START scores calculated from global data. These are then fine-tuned with local information that confirms which species and which threats are actually present. With that checked picture in hand, organisations can set firm targets, written as target START scores, and later track their progress using realised scores.

The idea is to bring the worldwide aim of halting species extinctions by 2050 down to earth. That aim sits within the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework. The adjusted scores also help show where threats need tackling after they appear and where they should be headed off in advance.

STAR has already had its first on-the-ground outing, in northern San José, Costa Rica. The scientists found that the best chance of lowering extinction risk there lay in dealing with habitat loss and degradation. The main causes were ranching and other livestock farming, growing urban areas, and invasive non-native species.

Dr Louise Mair, the paper's lead author and a research fellow at Newcastle, said the framework gives explicit recognition to "just how globally significant local conservation action can be". Professor Phil McGowan, who co-chairs the IUCN working group on STAR, observed that the metric appeared only five years ago, yet has seen considerable scientific development and take-up by businesses and others.

Dr Thomas Brooks, IUCN's chief scientist, said the framework offers conservationists a means of turning worldwide ambitions into measurable steps in a given place, with clearer priorities and stronger targets.

## Why it matters

International agreements to protect nature are easier to honour when progress can be measured, and that means linking what happens in a single catchment or landscape to the bigger goal. This framework sets out one way of making that connection, from checking local threats to setting targets and tracking results over time.
