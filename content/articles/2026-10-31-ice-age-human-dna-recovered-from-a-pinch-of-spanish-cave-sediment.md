Scientists have pulled the genetic traces of several ice age people out of a tiny scrap of cave sediment in Spain, even though the layer it came from holds no bones, teeth or fossils of any kind.

The sample came from El Mirón Cave and was barely heavier than a few grains of rice. It had to be sequenced 100 times to give enough usable data, and the team then waded through more than a billion DNA fragments to find human traces. They also had to allow for modern contamination, which was no mean feat.

The work was led from the University of Vienna and appears in the journal Science Advances. The genetic material was probably shed as skin, hair or blood by people taking shelter in the cave during the coldest part of Europe's last ice age. It seems to come from a mixed group, with more men than women.

The DNA suggests these people descended from a western ancestry group and probably had dark skin, hair and eyes. Skeletons from this period are exceptionally rare, and two of the few known remains from this culture are in very poor condition.

Researchers had already found animal and human mitochondrial DNA in the cave's dirt, reported in 2025. This time they recovered nuclear DNA, which comes from both parents and carries far more information about ancestry than mitochondrial DNA, which passes down only the maternal line.

Lead author Pere Gelabert and colleagues compared the results with a woman from France who lived about 31,000 years ago, and with a tooth found in northern Spain and dated to around 25,000 years ago. Both showed some Eastern ancestry, which hints that peoples from opposite ends of Europe were mixing before the last glacial maximum.

The El Mirón DNA, by contrast, showed western ancestry alone. The authors say the data argue against a major population replacement or break. Instead, the people of south-western Europe, from around 30,000 years ago to about 20,000 years ago, seem to have been a fairly continuous population.

Sediment DNA has practically doubled the evidence available for this time and place, a cracking result from such a small scoop of earth.

## Why it matters

Ancient human remains are scarce, and that has long limited what archaeologists can learn about our distant ancestors. Recovering DNA directly from sediment suggests that caves without a single bone could still reveal who lived there and how groups were related and moved about. The authors say more skeletal genomes and sediment datasets will be needed, but the method could open up sites once thought silent.
