A huge charity shop in Sutton Coldfield is giving young people who struggle to find work a supported route into employment. ReUsers, run by the social enterprise Jericho, is a "charity department store" with sections for furniture, tech, clothes and toys, plus a room given over entirely to Christmas decorations.

Demi Laville, 18, started an apprenticeship there six months ago. Before that, she was one of the young people not in employment, education or training, known as NEETs. She says she found it hard to get a foot in the door because employers seemed to expect experience she had no way of gaining.

The apprenticeship has already improved her communication and customer service skills. She hopes to move into property management one day and sees the job as a stepping stone.

Jericho runs seven non-profit businesses for people who face barriers to work. Next door to ReUsers, its Wood Shack crafts items from reclaimed wood. Many of its trainees are survivors of modern slavery, and manager Jon Owens says the six-month placements give isolated people company and something purposeful to do.

Long-term success stories are not hard to find. Mohzam Malik began as an apprentice at ReUsers 11 years ago, moved into electronics after showing an interest, and now runs that department. Staff also describe an apprentice with learning disabilities who went on to work for the NHS, and another who had avoided school exams and reached the equivalent of Level 4 in GCSE maths.

Chief executive Katie Webb says the approach is simple. "We just don't give up on people," she says. Apprentices typically leave 15 months after arriving, which she describes as ready to take on the world.

Keeping that going has not been easy. Jericho faced a £140,000 rise in running costs after increases to the apprentice wage, the national minimum wage and employer national insurance. Social investment backing helped it through without cutting the quality of its work, according to director Rebecca Stone.

## Why it matters

Figures for April to June 2026 show 981,000 people aged 16 to 24 were NEET, about 13% of that age group. A government-commissioned review has just proposed legal guarantees of support and a route into work, education or training. Webb argues that intensive placements like Jericho's are slow and not cheap, but can change the course of a life, and she wants them recognised as part of the wider system of support.
