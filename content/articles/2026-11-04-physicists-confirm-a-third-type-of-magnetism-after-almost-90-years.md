Physicists have confirmed that magnetism comes in a third form, ending almost nine decades in which only two main types were recognised. A team led by the US Department of Energy's SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory reports firm experimental evidence for altermagnetism in the journal Science.

Until now, the textbook categories were ferromagnets and antiferromagnets. In a ferromagnet, electron spins point the same way, which produces the field of an everyday magnet. In an antiferromagnet, a class identified in 1933, neighbouring spins point in opposite directions and their effects cancel out.

Altermagnets also alternate their spins, so they show no overall magnetisation. The difference is that the crystal structures around the opposing spins are rotated relative to one another. That breaks a symmetry found in ordinary antiferromagnets and changes how electrons move. As a result, these materials can carry spin currents, which pass information along using spin rather than electrical charge alone.

The researchers studied manganese telluride, a material long regarded as a standard antiferromagnet. They searched for chirality, a form of handedness. An object is chiral if its mirror image cannot be made to sit exactly on the original, as your left and right hands show.

The experiments took place at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility in France. The team used X-rays whose electric fields twist in one of two opposite directions as the light travels. Chiral materials scatter the two beams differently, while non-chiral ones treat them alike. Manganese telluride showed a clear difference, exactly the signal expected from altermagnetism and one that a conventional antiferromagnet would not produce.

The first run came in 2024. The team returned in early 2025, repeated the measurements and reproduced the results, a cracking way to firm up the case.

Lead author Daniel Jost, of SLAC and the Stanford Institute for Materials and Energy Sciences, explained that by 2024 there were strong hints but one piece of the puzzle was still missing. The chiral signature, he said, supplies the solid ground for treating altermagnetism as a category of its own. Co-author Rachel Nickel, a postdoctoral scientist with the European facility, noted that the idea was only proposed a few years ago.

## Why it matters

Spintronics, a growing field that uses electron spin as well as charge, could gain from materials that pair the stability of antiferromagnets with the information-carrying talent of spin currents. According to the researchers, devices built this way could run faster, cope better with interference and use substantially less electricity. That points towards better memory, quantum devices and more energy-efficient data centres, although those applications have yet to be developed.
