Sockeye salmon can swim into Okanagan Lake again, for the first time in close to a century, thanks to a new passage that carries them past the dam at Penticton. The Okanagan Nation Alliance led the project, which crowns a long effort driven by Syilx Okanagan communities.

Over the past hundred years, dams and channel engineering shut salmon out of more than 350 square kilometres of lake habitat. The Okanagan Lake Dam, finished in 1953, was among the last big obstacles. An earlier fishway there never worked properly, and for much of the year the water levels left it useless to salmon.

The replacement is a nature-like channel rather than a metal fish ladder. Open grating overhead floods the water with daylight, and visitors can look down to see fish moving through beneath them.

Steelhead, rainbow trout, sockeye, chinook and kokanee can all use the route to reach the lake and 13 tributary streams. A built-in adjustable barrier is designed to stop invasive species from following, so only native fish get through.

The Alliance represents eight member communities and runs one of the country's most extensive Indigenous-led fisheries programmes, covering hatcheries, habitat repair and changes to dams. Its salmon recovery work stretches back to the 1990s and includes a sockeye reintroduction in Skaha Lake that ran for 12 years, plus around 50 habitat projects in the basin. Alterations to two other dams further downstream have already opened up extra spawning grounds.

In 2026 alone, 330,000 sockeye fry from the Alliance's hatchery were released across the territory, with students joining some of the releases to learn about salmon biology and Syilx culture. Securing a permanent route past the dam is no mean feat after so many years.

Singing and drumming marked the opening, and speakers paid tribute to the leaders who pressed for the passage over many years. Elders describe a bond with salmon built on responsibility rather than simply using a resource.

Elsewhere, recovery is on the up. The Alliance's fishing crew has landed record numbers of returning sockeye on Osoyoos Lake in recent years, with thousands of fish shared among member communities. Returns to Okanagan Lake are expected to be modest at first, because warm river conditions have held numbers back lately.

## Why it matters

Before dams and river channelling broke up their habitat, Okanagan salmon runs were counted in the hundreds of thousands. A passage that stays open for every future migration season gives the fish a lasting chance to rebuild. Okanagan-origin fish also make up the largest share of sockeye returning most years to the Columbia River system shared by Canada and the United States, so the benefits reach well beyond British Columbia. The project has also been nominated for an international fish passage award, a sign of growing interest in Indigenous-led river restoration.
