Doctors in eastern England have shown that a small balloon, inflated inside the body's largest artery, can swiftly raise blood pressure in people whose hearts have not responded to standard resuscitation. The early findings were shared in Chicago at a resuscitation science meeting run by the American Heart Association.

Only around one in ten people survive a cardiac arrest that happens outside hospital. Lead author James Price, a consultant emergency physician at Cambridge University Hospitals and research lead at the East Anglian Air Ambulance, points out that survival rates and treatment options have barely moved in half a century.

His team borrowed a method normally used to control life-threatening internal bleeding, known as REBOA. A slim balloon is passed through a groin artery and inflated in the upper aorta, which temporarily steers blood towards the heart and brain.

Air ambulance doctors tried it at roadsides and in patients' homes once other treatments had failed, including high-quality CPR, medication and a breathing tube. Over two years, they managed to block the aorta in 33 of 36 patients. The procedure took about seven minutes from start to finish, which is no mean feat at an emergency scene.

Blood pressure measures improved sharply. The lower, diastolic reading, which matters for restarting the heart, jumped by 59%, from 26 to 42 mm Hg. The upper, systolic reading rose by 20% on average, and carbon dioxide in exhaled breath, a sign of better circulation, rose by 50%. No serious adverse effects were linked to the procedure.

Eight patients regained a sustained pulse, and two left hospital with good brain function. Among five patients whose arrest was witnessed and caused by ventricular fibrillation, a dangerous heart rhythm, and who received the balloon somewhat sooner, four regained a sustained pulse and two went home with good neurological outcomes.

Price was careful about the limits. There was no comparison group, and the study was not designed to show whether survival improves. He called it "an important first step", but not yet proof that REBOA saves lives.

The next stage is to work out which patients benefit most and when the balloon should be used. Price also suggests looking at whether specially trained responders who are not doctors, such as advanced paramedics, could offer it earlier.

## Why it matters

Cardiac arrest outside hospital remains one of medicine's toughest challenges, and options are scarce once standard resuscitation fails. This small study suggests the technique can be delivered quickly and safely in the field, and that it improves blood flow measures. Larger trials with comparison groups must now test whether that means more lives saved.
