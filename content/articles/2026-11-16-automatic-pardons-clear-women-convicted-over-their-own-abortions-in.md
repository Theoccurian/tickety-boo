Women in England and Wales who were arrested or convicted over ending their own pregnancies outside the law are having their criminal records wiped, as automatic pardons take effect. Nobody has to apply or take any step to benefit.

The move draws a line under a Victorian-era law that reached women themselves. Tonia Antoniazzi, the Labour MP who tabled the decriminalisation amendment last year, says more than 100 women will now have their names cleared after being needlessly harmed by a dated piece of legislation.

The reform comes hot on the heels of the case of Nicola Packer. In 2020, during a Covid lockdown, she was 41 and was prescribed two medicines, mifepristone and misoprostol, over the phone to end what she believed was an early pregnancy. She in fact had a cryptic pregnancy, one with no outward signs, and was around 26 weeks along, well beyond the 10-week limit for taking such pills at home.

Ms Packer had a stillbirth and went to hospital, where staff alerted the police. Her friend Helle Tumbridge told the BBC's Today programme that she was arrested and recovered with two officers in her room. Prosecutors alleged she had known she was more than 10 weeks pregnant, which she denied.

Five years of investigations passed before the case came to trial. A jury then deliberated for more than six hours and unanimously found her not guilty. By then, Ms Tumbridge says, the damage had been done.

The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists said at the time that the trial laid bare the harm the law could cause. Under the new rules, a woman ending her own pregnancy at any stage no longer commits an offence.

Minister Alex Davies-Jones said pardoning these women had been a priority. Those affected can read guidance online or contact a dedicated casework team, though they do not have to do either.

Heidi Stewart, of the abortion care provider BPAS, said no pardon could undo the distress these women had suffered, but called the change "a meaningful step towards justice".

## Why it matters

A criminal record can follow someone for years, and the women involved went through investigation and prosecution at moments of real personal pain. Clearing their names automatically, rather than asking them to apply, spares them from reliving it all over again. The rules on abortion care itself are unchanged: providers still work to the same time limits, with narrow exceptions after 24 weeks. What shifts is that women are no longer treated as criminals for their own pregnancies, which is rather good news.
