Two female gorillas at Ramat Gan Safari in Israel have been filmed making a "living ladder": one stands steady while the other climbs onto her back to seize foliage that is otherwise too high. Researchers at Tel Aviv University describe the behaviour in a paper in the journal Primates, calling it a rare case of spontaneous cooperation between adult gorillas.

The two are western lowland gorillas and half-sisters, Amelia and Lisha. Three episodes were recorded, in 2021, 2024 and 2025, and each gorilla kept to the same job every time. Lisha acted as the firm base, while Amelia did the climbing and reaching. Nobody had taught them the trick.

The fullest record dates from September 2024. Keepers noticed the sisters calmly feeding on bark and foliage from a big branch that had previously been out of bounds, so staff checked security camera footage to find out how. Amelia went to a rock beneath a tree, Lisha joined her and planted herself on all fours, and Amelia then stood upright on her back and broke off a branch roughly 4.5 metres (15 feet) up. An electric barrier around the trunk ruled out climbing the tree itself.

A video from June 2025 added a telling detail. Lisha walked over to Amelia and presented her back, ready for the climb. Amelia was not seen pushing or manoeuvring her, and Lisha seemed to take part of her own accord. The team reads this as genuine cooperation, since each gorilla's actions relied on the other's and were aimed at the same immediate goal.

Working together did not always mean sharing the spoils. After the 2024 event, keepers reported a peaceful shared meal, but in 2025 Amelia ate the vegetation by herself. The researchers take this as a sign that cooperating to get food and then sharing it are separate parts of social life.

It is a cracking example of teamwork all the more because the sisters are not especially close. They are housed apart overnight and seldom groom each other. Study leader Michael Levitzky argues that Lisha was no mere step, but an active and willing partner who chose her own position without being guided.

The researchers are candid about the limits. The study rests on two gorillas and three events, and the footage cannot show whether the pair used sounds or subtle gestures, or planned ahead. They suggest that systematic filming and audio recording could help to explain how the animals coordinate.

## Why it matters

Gorillas have long been regarded as less cognitively sophisticated than other great apes, and careful documentation of behaviour like this helps to test that view. The study suggests that real teamwork can arise between animals that are not close companions, and without language or advance planning. The researchers say this offers a partial clue to how early humans relied on cooperation to survive, and it gives keepers and scientists a firmer footing for understanding gorillas' social world.
