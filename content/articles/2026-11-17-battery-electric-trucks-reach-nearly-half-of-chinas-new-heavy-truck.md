Nearly one in two heavy lorries newly registered in China in September 2026 ran on batteries. Of 65,800 registrations that month, 32,800 were battery-electric, giving a 49.8% share, while just 36 used hydrogen fuel cells.

The pace of change is striking. Battery power took only around 13% of the domestic heavy-truck market in 2024, then 28% in 2025, and nearly 38% across the first nine months of 2026. The figures cover trucks sold within China rather than exports, and the wider truck market is still smaller than before the pandemic, so this is a story of shifting share rather than booming sales.

New-energy heavy trucks, a group that is almost entirely battery-electric, have held more than 40% of new registrations every month from May to September. About 226,400 were registered in the first nine months, a rise of 64% on the same stretch of 2025. Tractor units, which pull semi-trailers, are setting the pace: in September, 64.48% of new registrations of this type were new-energy models.

Running costs are a big part of the appeal. In case studies from the ICCT, a 28-strong electric fleet based at a Guangdong warehouse was estimated to cost 16% less to own over five years than comparable diesels. One operation covering 900 km a day, backed by well-sited high-power charging, came out 19% cheaper. A 50-vehicle express-delivery fleet in the Yangtze River Delta managed a slimmer 3% saving over six years, and that was without purchase incentives.

Infrastructure is being laid where the freight already travels. In 2025, Yunnan brought 3,350 km of charging routes for heavy lorries into service, served by 113 stations. In October 2026, Fujian launched a 649 km coastal stretch with 15 charging stations and eight battery-swap stations. Swap-capable trucks were 20.09% of September's battery-electric registrations.

Hydrogen has gained little ground. Only 689 fuel-cell heavy trucks were registered in the first nine months, set against more than 223,000 battery-electric ones.

Kpler projects that new-energy trucks will be about 8% of heavy trucks in service by the end of 2026, displacing roughly 230,000 barrels of diesel a day. China's national plan aims for these vehicles to reach a 40% share of new heavy-truck sales by 2030, and monthly sales have already cleared that bar, a cracking result. The target for the wider fleet will take longer to reach.

## Why it matters

Heavy lorries travel enormous distances and burn a great deal of fuel, so even a minority switching to electric can make a noticeable dent in diesel demand and tailpipe pollution. China's figures suggest that when routes, charging and finance are planned together, battery trucks can compete on cost as well as on cleanliness. The climate benefit still depends on how the electricity is generated, but the direction of travel is encouraging.
