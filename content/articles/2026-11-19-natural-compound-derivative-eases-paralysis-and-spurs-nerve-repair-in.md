A compound made from a natural substance has eased paralysis and activated the nervous system's own repair pathways in early laboratory studies of multiple sclerosis (MS). The work is preclinical and the compound has not yet been tested in people, but its creators think it is a worthwhile drug candidate for further study.

The compound, called furapyrrodole (FP), was developed by a team at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign working with colleagues at Keio University and Chiba University in Japan. Their findings appear in the journal Advanced Science.

In MS, the immune system attacks the myelin sheaths that insulate nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord. Current treatments can curb relapses and slow the disease, but none can properly bring back nerve function once it has been lost.

The researchers wanted to nudge stem and progenitor cells, which remain in the adult brain and spinal cord, into becoming neurons and myelin-making cells. Standing in the way is a signalling system called Notch, which discourages that change. Drugs that block Notch directly are known to upset the gut, so the team went looking for a more roundabout approach.

They screened a chemical library at Chiba and spotted useful activity in a family of compounds known as spirooxindoles. After making and testing a series of derivatives, they settled on FP as the strongest. It boosts a mitochondrial enzyme called CYB5R3, which lowers calcium levels inside the cell. That in turn quietens another enzyme that Notch signalling depends on.

In the laboratory studies, FP brought better movement and less paralysis. The spinal cord held more motor neurons and more oligodendrocytes, the cells that help rebuild myelin, while inflammation in the central nervous system was dampened.

No intestinal side effects appeared. The researchers explain that CYB5R3 is plentiful in neural progenitor cells but present only in very small amounts in the intestine.

More work is needed on how well FP works, how safe it is and how it behaves as a medicine. The team also sees possible uses in other neurological conditions, including Alzheimer's disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.

## Why it matters

Holding MS in check is one thing; repairing the damage it leaves behind is quite another, and it remains a big gap in modern medicine. A treatment that encourages the body's own cells to rebuild nerve tissue, without the gut troubles linked to other approaches, would be rather good news if it holds up. It is early days, and many safety checks lie ahead, but the approach gives researchers a fresh direction.
