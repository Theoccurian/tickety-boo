Ice buried in the Guliya ice cap, in northwestern Tibet, may be more than 128,000 years old, according to a new study. If the dating stands up, it is a frozen survivor of the last ice age. It pushes the known glacial record for this part of Tibet back by more than 100,000 years, and it is the oldest ice ever recovered there.

The research was led by Lonnie Thompson, a professor of earth sciences at Ohio State University. His team compared cores drilled on the Guliya Plateau in 1992 and 2015. Even though they were taken 23 years apart, both showed very close oxygen isotope signals, which are chemical clues to conditions in the past. Such a match suggests the ice has kept a dependable record that can be reproduced.

Putting an age on ice that has stayed frozen for so long is no mean feat. The international team turned to radioactive forms of beryllium and chlorine, which decay at predictable rates. With them, they picked out the chemical fingerprint of a dramatic upset in Earth's magnetic field more than 41,000 years ago.

They then set their results beside oxygen isotope records from nearby Tibetan cave deposits. Taken together, the evidence suggests the Guliya ice reaches well beyond 128,000 years.

Earlier work had indicated that the ice cap's record began only in the middle of the Holocene, the epoch that started nearly 12,000 years ago. The new findings show that part of the ice is far older than that.

Professor Thompson points out that, outside the polar regions, this is the only mountaintop ice core known to stretch back so far. Cores from Huascarán, by comparison, go back only a little over 30,000 years.

The team now plans to study more samples from Guliya. With the age of the ice better understood, other indicators of past climate and environment may be read in a fresh light, and details once overlooked could come to the fore. The findings appear in the journal Science Advances. The work was backed by the U.S. National Science Foundation, with co-authors from the United States, Switzerland and China.

## Why it matters

An ice core is only as useful as its timeline, because scientists need to know when each layer formed to see how the climate shifted. A secure age for the Guliya ice also shows that large ice masses in Tibet lasted through the early Holocene instead of vanishing as the last ice age ended. That is a welcome boost for researchers trying to work out how glaciers cope with big swings in climate, and when past glaciations began and ended.
