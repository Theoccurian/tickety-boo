An experimental blood test from the University of Notre Dame can screen for four serious diseases in about an hour, using only a tiny sample. The conditions are colorectal cancer, glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer and Alzheimer's disease.

The patent-pending platform was developed by a team led by Hsueh-Chia Chang, a professor of engineering at the university. The researchers tried it out blind on 87 plasma samples, drawn from patients who were healthy and from others who had one of the four diseases. Its accuracy for each condition came in between 90% and 99%. The findings appear in Nature Biomedical Engineering.

The test hunts for exosomes, minute structures in the blood that carry proteins and can offer an early sign that something is wrong. To catch them, Chang's laboratory created what it calls immunojanus particles. These are tiny spheres with two different sides: one is coated with antibodies, and the other glows.

Each test needs just 10 microlitres of blood. It is thinned with saline and left to mix with the particles for between 10 and 60 minutes, during which the antibodies latch onto proteins that are specific to a disease and sit on the outside of exosomes.

The mixture then goes under a microscope, where the particles show up as flickering points of light, a little like a starry sky. A bespoke computer program tracks their jittery, unpredictable motion, known as Brownian motion, to establish whether a marker of disease is present.

Chang explains that the particles manage to cut through interference because they are both sensitive and selective. A single exosome on a particle can be picked up from how often it blinks, while other small molecules give off no signal at all.

Compared with the leading exosome-detection technology, the new test is judged to be a good deal more accurate. It has also been deliberately designed to be quick and inexpensive.

## Why it matters

Finding serious illness earlier often gives doctors more options, and Chang hopes that earlier screening could improve patients' chances of survival. A single, speedy and affordable test that can flag several different diseases would be a welcome boost for diagnostics. It is still experimental, though, and was checked on a fairly small set of samples, so further study would be needed before it could be used in routine care.
