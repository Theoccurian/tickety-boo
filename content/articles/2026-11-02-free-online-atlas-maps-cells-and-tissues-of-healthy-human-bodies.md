Researchers across the United States have opened a free online portal to one of the most comprehensive collections of information on healthy human cells, tissues and organ systems. Described on 2 November in the journal Nature Communications, it is meant to help scientists, and artificial intelligence projects, understand health and disease.

The portal is the public face of the Human BioMolecular Atlas Program, or HuBMAP, an eight-year undertaking involving more than 600 consortium members at over 60 institutions. Its holdings span upwards of 9,000 datasets contributed by more than 500 healthy donors, covering dozens of organs and tissues. The team says no other single resource combines that breadth and size.

Visitors can browse by organ system, look up which genes are active in different tissues, and dig into single-cell information from a wide range of cell populations. Built-in web tools allow quick analysis of image-based, spatial and biomolecular data. In all, the collection pools more than 25 kinds of imaging and molecular measurement.

Making it all fit together was no mean feat. The samples had been gathered using many different technologies, so the portal team agreed shared standards for data and descriptive metadata with other consortium members. They then built standardised processing pipelines, so that results from many laboratories are treated in the same way and can be compared fairly.

The portal also lets researchers judge whether the atlas holds anything relevant to their question without first downloading enormous files or using heavy computing power. For deeper work, data can be downloaded, and the metadata and programming interfaces are openly available. Protected material, such as sequencing data that might identify a donor, requires approval.

First author Morgan Turner, of Harvard Medical School's HIDIVE Lab, said that knowing what healthy tissue looks like has to come before understanding illness. Senior author Nils Gehlenborg said the tools let users explore relationships among the trillions of cells in a human body in a way not previously possible.

The build was led by teams at Harvard Medical School, Stanford, the University of Pennsylvania, Carnegie Mellon, the University of Pittsburgh and the Pittsburgh Supercomputing Center. The last two are also responsible for the computing infrastructure and for keeping the resource available.

## Why it matters

A growing number of biomedical atlases record the cellular and chemical状態 of people living with particular diseases, but the researchers say a healthy sample is needed to complete the picture. A single, openly shared reference of normal tissue, processed to common standards, lets scientists compare findings across laboratories, organs and donors. Because the data are formatted to be ready for AI, it may also feed into computer-based research.
