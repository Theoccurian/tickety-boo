The BepiColombo spacecraft, a joint European and Japanese venture, has reached Mercury after travelling more than 6bn miles over upwards of eight years. On Saturday, flight controllers will fire its thrusters to nudge it into orbit around the smallest planet in the solar system.

Only one mission has ever circled Mercury before, so BepiColombo will be a rare second. Getting there is no mean feat. Mercury lies so deep in the sun's gravity that spacecraft keep speeding up on approach, and they have to brake for much of the trip or they would simply sail past.

The €1.6bn (£1.4bn) project gives researchers their best chance yet of learning how Mercury came to form so near the sun and why it has a magnetic field. It should also help identify what the ice in the planet's permanently shadowed craters is made of.

"We're very excited, it's been a long wait," said Prof Emma Bunce. She is based at Leicester University and has been involved for two decades, leading work on an X-ray instrument that will study the chemistry of the planet's surface.

There is still some patience required. In early December, the craft will divide into two separate orbiters, one European and one Japanese, each heading for its own polar orbit. The research itself will not begin until next April.

The European orbiter will chart the surface and its make-up in fine detail, and probe the interior to learn more about Mercury's unusually large core. The Japanese orbiter will focus on the magnetic field and on the sodium exosphere, the wispy layer of gas and atoms surrounding the planet.

Another puzzle is density. Mercury is about half as big again as the moon, yet it holds four times as much mass, and the team hopes to find out why.

It is a world of extremes. One rotation takes 58 Earth days, so a spot on the surface can bask in sunshine for three months, then endure three months of darkness. Daytime ground temperatures reach 450C, while nights plunge to -180C.

Prof Geraint Jones, lead project scientist at the European Space Agency, said the prospect of results had kept the team enthused throughout the voyage. After roughly three years in orbit the spacecraft will run out of fuel and eventually crash into the surface.

## Why it matters

Mercury is the closest planet to the sun and remains barely explored, with just one earlier spacecraft ever having orbited it. Two orbiters carrying a wide range of instruments will study its surface, interior, magnetic field and surrounding space together, tackling questions that scientists have had to leave open until now.
