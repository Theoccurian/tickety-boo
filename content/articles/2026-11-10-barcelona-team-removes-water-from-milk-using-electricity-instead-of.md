Scientists in Barcelona have shown that an electric field can pull a large share of the water out of milk at room temperature, with none of the fierce heat normally used to make milk powder. In early laboratory tests, the milk's water content dropped by nearly half, and its main proteins appeared to come through unchanged.

Making powdered milk the conventional way is effective but thirsty for energy. The high temperatures can also harm delicate ingredients, including whey proteins, enzymes and vitamins. Even so, powder remains a favourite of the food industry, since it keeps for long periods and travels without refrigeration.

The work was a joint effort between a Barcelona microelectronics institute and a food research and innovation centre at the Autonomous University of Barcelona. Lead author Bernat Perez Playà and three colleagues describe it in the journal Innovative Food Science & Emerging Technologies. It is the first time the technique, called electrospray, has been tried on milk, and the method is now being patented.

Left to itself, a liquid holds together thanks to surface tension. A strong electric field overcomes that, splitting the milk into millions of minuscule droplets from which water escapes far more easily, even at 20°C.

The team worked with reconstituted skimmed milk, applying 29 kV with a 10 cm gap between the tip and the collector. How slowly the milk was fed in made all the difference. At a brisk 0.2 mL/min, the solids content hardly shifted. At a gentle 0.01 mL/min, milk starting at 12.3% solids reached 36.8%, which is no mean feat for a first attempt. The researchers explain that slower flow gives each droplet more charge for its size, helping it break apart and shed water.

To check the proteins, the team used gel electrophoresis, a laboratory method for examining their structure. The results suggest that the caseins and whey proteins were left unaltered.

The authors are admirably frank that this is only a first step. No completely dry powder was produced, and processing capacity, energy efficiency and scalability still need proving. Next, they plan to fine-tune flow rate, voltage and droplet travel distance, test set-ups with several emission points, and assess energy use and microbiological stability.

## Why it matters

Heat-based drying uses a great deal of energy and can strip away some of the qualities that make food worth eating. A gentler, room-temperature method could, if it scales up, yield stable, easy-to-store products that stay closer to the original. The researchers also see promise for other heat-sensitive liquids such as juices, though industrial use is still some way off.
