Chemists have created an AI assistant that lets researchers describe the enzyme they have in mind in everyday language and get a workable plan back. The tool, MutexaGPT, comes from a team led by Zhongyue (John) Yang, an assistant professor of chemistry. Qianzhen Shao, who did doctoral work under Yang and is now a postdoctoral researcher at Stanford, helped build it alongside other colleagues. The study is published in Nature Computational Science.

The system works in stages. It takes a plain-English request, uses language model agents to chase up anything the scientist has left unsaid, builds physics-based models, and then turns the results into concrete design proposals.

Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions, which is why Yang describes them as nature's catalysts. Each is built from hundreds of amino acids, and swapping just one, known as a mutation, can change its behaviour considerably. Nobody can yet reliably predict which mutations will help, so industry leans heavily on trial and error. That means a lot of expensive testing.

MutexaGPT is designed to cut down that guesswork. One use is cold-active enzymes, which keep working efficiently at lower temperatures. These matter for lactose-free milk and for preserving food without damaging heat-sensitive nutrients. They also make possible detergents that shift complicated stains without hot water.

Drug manufacturing could gain as well. Enzymes that are more efficient and more selective can make pharmaceutical production more sustainable, by using less energy, creating less chemical waste and avoiding harsh reaction conditions.

The name is a contraction of Alexa for Mutant, a nod to the team's original idea of a conversational helper for designing enzyme mutants. The GPT part refers to the language models that let scientists put questions to powerful computational tools in ordinary words.

Yang says he shares the public's worries about AI pushing people out of work. Here, he argues, the technology does the opposite: it takes the imaginative instincts of scientists and shapes them into something usable. Specialists often develop strong principles of their own without knowing how to put numbers on them, and this is where the tool steps in.

Yang grew up in China and studied at Nankai University before starting at UCLA in 2013. After a postdoctoral fellowship at MIT, he joined his present faculty in 2020 and has been developing Mutexa ever since.

## Why it matters

Enzymes underpin medicines, food production and plastic recycling, so anything that makes them quicker and cheaper to improve is a welcome boost. Less trial and error should mean less wasted energy and material along the way. It is also encouraging to see an AI tool built to draw on human creativity rather than sideline it.
