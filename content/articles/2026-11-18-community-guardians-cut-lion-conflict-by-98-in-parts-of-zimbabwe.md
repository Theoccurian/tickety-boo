A Zimbabwean conservation charity has recorded a 98% fall in conflict between people and lions in the two wards where it first started work, by training local people to act as community guardians.

Wildlife Conservation Action began in two wards of Nyaminyami rural district six years ago. Across the districts of Mbire and Binga, where it has also been active, conflict incidents have dropped by 53%, which is a cracking result for such a young organisation.

The guardians, known as "Batabilili", are recruited from the communities they serve and trained by the charity. Their tasks include helping farmers secure livestock in above-ground kraals, keeping track of where lions are moving and holding community meetings. The charity also uses tracking collars and school programmes, and the guardians offer emotional and psychological support to people who have lost animals.

The charity's founder, conservation biologist Moreangels Mbizah, says local roots are the secret. "They are trusted because they come from these communities," she says.

That trust matters because villagers have often felt that wildlife authorities care about animals but not about the people living beside them. Mbizah says this sense of being ignored helped fuel retaliatory killings of lions that prey on livestock. Having a trusted neighbour on hand to deal with their concerns has helped reduce such killings.

Mbizah came to the work after a career studying African wild dogs and lions, including time in Hwange National Park. About ten years in, she felt there was a gap: communities needed a bigger part in conservation, and help with the effects of lions on their livelihoods.

She also runs a mentorship programme for female university students. She says it grew partly from her own experience as one of the few Black Zimbabwean women in conservation when she started, when she struggled to find mentors or role models.

This year the Whitley Fund for Nature, a UK charity, gave her a Whitley Award, often called the "Green Oscar". Mbizah says organisations in neighbouring countries are already talking to her about the guardian model.

## Why it matters

Lions have disappeared from much of their former range, and killings in revenge for lost livestock are one reason. Protecting them depends heavily on the people who share the land with them. This approach shows that when communities are listened to, given practical tools and supported by someone they trust, coexistence becomes more realistic. If other countries adopt the model, the benefits could reach well beyond Zimbabwe.
