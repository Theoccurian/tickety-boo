Scientists have identified two howler monkey species that were previously unknown to science, both living in the Amazon rainforest. Named Alouatta iroi and Alouatta matogrossensis, they come to light 85 years after the last howler species was formally described, which is rather good news for anyone who cares about the forest's wealth of wildlife.

The work was led by the University of Salford with international partners. The team examined genome-wide data from 11 howler species, drawn from locations stretching from South to Central America, and set it alongside physical evidence from museum specimens. The result is the fullest evolutionary family tree for howler monkeys yet produced. It showed that a group once treated as a single species in fact contained several distinct lineages.

The study also sheds light on how these primates spread. Great rivers, among them the Amazon, the Rio Branco and the Xingu, appear to have acted as barriers for millions of years. With populations held apart, they slowly diverged into separate species on opposite banks and in isolated pockets of forest.

Published in Communications Biology, the findings help to close two long-standing gaps in biology: how many species really exist, and where each of them lives.

There is a note of caution, too. The researchers flagged several populations that may be at serious risk of extinction, including newly recognised species that have never had a conservation assessment. Alouatta matogrossensis occurs in southern Amazonia, a region considered one of the fastest-deforesting in the world, where the spread of cropland and other pressures are shrinking and splintering its habitat.

Professor Jean Boubli, of the University of Salford, said the discovery arrives at a critical moment, with deforestation and climate change threatening Amazonian species before science has fully understood them. He hopes the results will help conservation groups pick out the most vulnerable populations and decide where to focus protection.

His colleague Dr Romina Batista noted that even well-known animals such as howler monkeys can still hold surprises, and that knowing which species exist is where protection begins.

The Reconecta Project, led by conservationist Fernanda Abra, is one initiative with a related aim. It seeks to reduce road deaths among tree-dwelling animals by linking fragments of forest, with the newly described Mato Grosso red howler monkey among the species it hopes to help.

## Why it matters

Conservation depends on knowing what exists and where it lives, because a species that has not been recognised cannot be formally assessed or protected. Finding two new primates within a group as familiar as howler monkeys suggests the Amazon still harbours a great deal of undescribed life. Putting names and maps to these animals gives conservationists firmer footing while their forests are still standing.
