Holding a small baby skin-to-skin against a parent's chest probably saves lives, according to an updated Cochrane review. The strongest signs of benefit came from places where resources are limited.

The researchers brought together 49 randomised trials covering 7,724 newborns who were born early or weighed very little at birth. That is more than twice as many studies as the 2016 edition, which helped to shape World Health Organization guidelines.

Kangaroo care is rather more than a cuddle. It means early, continuous and lengthy skin-to-skin contact, regular exclusive breastfeeding and, where suitable, an early journey home from hospital. Compared with standard newborn care, it probably lowers the chance of a baby dying in hospital and reduces infections picked up on the ward, including sepsis. It may also encourage exclusive breastfeeding and mean fewer episodes of dangerously low body temperature.

Dr Emma Olsson of Örebro University in Sweden, the review's first author, said the extra trials strengthen the case for an idea that has been around for decades. The fall in deaths among some of the most vulnerable newborns was, she said, the most striking finding.

The approach began as an inexpensive option for places short of equipment and money, and it is now used around the world. Much of the evidence comes from low- and middle-income countries, which is where the benefits looked greatest.

The authors are admirably frank about the gaps. Deaths fell in every kind of setting, but they were less sure of the evidence in wealthy countries, where intensive care units already see very few deaths. More work is needed to learn how well the results carry over.

Nor is it known how much is enough. WHO guidance suggests at least eight hours a day, yet the review found too little evidence to say whether longer brings extra benefit. "We know that kangaroo care is beneficial," said co-author Dr Roger Soll of the University of Vermont, who added that the open questions are how much, and how best to deliver it.

The team also points out that long daily spells of skin-to-skin contact may not be realistic for every parent. Future studies, they say, should look at which babies gain most and how to make the method work for different families and health systems.

## Why it matters

Being born small or early remains a serious danger to newborns, and many hospitals around the world have little money or equipment to spare. A method that relies chiefly on a parent's presence, and now rests on a much larger body of trial evidence, offers a practical way to give fragile babies a better chance. The authors' honesty about what is still unknown should also help health services tailor care to the families they serve, which is no mean feat.
