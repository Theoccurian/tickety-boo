A new tablet has been approved in the United States for a form of advanced breast cancer that hormone drugs can no longer hold back. The Food and Drug Administration cleared giredestrant, sold as Wilserdly, on Monday. It is taken once a day alongside another cancer drug, everolimus.

The approval applies to adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative disease that has spread into nearby tissue or to other parts of the body. Only patients whose tumours carry a change in the ESR1 gene qualify, and their cancer must have returned or progressed after earlier hormone therapy. The manufacturer, Roche, says such mutations turn up in as many as four in ten people with this kind of advanced cancer, and they help tumours evade hormone drugs.

To pick out suitable patients, the FDA has also authorised a blood test, FoundationOne Liquid CDx. It looks for traces of tumour DNA carried in the blood.

The two medicines attack the cancer from different directions. Giredestrant binds to oestrogen receptors on tumour cells, blocking the hormone and prompting the receptors to be broken down. Everolimus shuts off a separate route that the cells depend on to multiply.

The decision rests on a trial involving 373 adults whose disease had advanced after previous treatment. Of these, 207 had ESR1 mutations. All of them received everolimus, paired with either giredestrant or a hormone therapy picked by their own doctor.

Among those with the mutation, people given giredestrant typically went 10 months before their disease progressed, against 5.5 months in the comparison group. Tumours shrank in 27% of the giredestrant group, compared with 14% of the others.

There are caveats. The FDA says it is not yet clear whether the pairing extends survival. Common side effects included inflammation of the mouth or lungs, rash, tiredness, diarrhoea, muscle and joint pain, and nausea. The label also carries warnings about a slow heartbeat and about risks to an unborn child.

Patients take 30 milligrams daily, with or without food. Roche has set a list price of about $270,000 a year. A separate FDA decision, due by 30 November, will consider the drug for early-stage disease after surgery in people at higher risk of recurrence.

## Why it matters

Once hormone treatments stop working, the choices open to people with advanced breast cancer tend to narrow, so a new oral option is a welcome boost. The approval also fits a wider move towards matching treatment to the genetic features of a patient's tumour, with a companion blood test used to find those who qualify. Several extra months before the disease progresses are meaningful for patients, although the effect on survival has yet to be established.
