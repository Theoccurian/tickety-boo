Twelve species of Acropora coral that were folded into other species decades ago have been reinstated, after a genetic analysis showed they were separate all along. The study, led by Augustine Crosbie of James Cook University, is published in the journal Invertebrate Systematics.

Its findings suggest the Great Barrier Reef is home to a wider variety of Acropora corals than anyone had appreciated. Four further corals, whose identities had been unresolved, have also now been recognised as valid species.

The tale begins with the methods of earlier scientists. In the 19th and 20th centuries, collectors brought up pieces of coral, left them in the sun and soaked them in household bleach to remove the living tissue. That left a hard skeleton, which researchers compared with a single "type specimen" to decide whether two corals were the same species.

Late in the 20th century, another group of scientists reviewed the archived specimens and judged that many of them overlapped too much to count as separate species. Plenty of names were merged. The trouble is that coral skeletons contain no DNA, so museum collections could not settle the matter.

To get genetic evidence, the researchers had to retrace the steps of their predecessors, going back to the places where the corals originally came from. It was a neat piece of scientific detective work. Specimens in the analysis came from the Great Barrier Reef, Fiji, Tonga, the Philippines and Singapore.

Peter Cowman, principal scientist at Queensland Museum, which holds one of the world's largest coral collections, says samples from those original locations often point to more species than the records allow. Today, a small branch is taken from each coral as a tissue sample and stored in ethanol in an ultra-low freezer, to be sequenced as resources allow.

There is a sobering note. Acropora corals have been hit particularly hard by bleaching in recent years, and more species could mean each one lives in a much smaller area, leaving it potentially more vulnerable.

## Why it matters

Protecting wildlife depends on knowing what exists. When several species are counted as one, the rarity of each can go unnoticed, and so can the danger it faces. Clearer species lists help scientists judge extinction risk, monitor reef communities and decide where limited conservation resources should go, which matters all the more for corals already struggling with bleaching.
