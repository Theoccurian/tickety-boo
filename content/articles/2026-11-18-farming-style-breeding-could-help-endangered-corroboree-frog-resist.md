A breeding method long familiar on farms may offer a way to help the southern corroboree frog stand up to the disease that has all but erased it from the wild, according to new Australian research.

The frog is classed as functionally extinct because of chytridiomycosis, an illness driven by a fungus. Breeding in captivity has kept this critically endangered species going, but releasing animals is a tall order while the fungus stays put in the landscape.

The study, published in npj Biodiversity, is described as the first time genomic selection has been used in conservation. Dr Mikaeylah Davidson, who did the work for her PhD at the University of Melbourne, found that the time a frog lasts after meeting the fungus varies from animal to animal. Part of that variation is down to genes that can be handed on to offspring.

That matters because it means breeders have something to work with. The team paired disease data with genetic information from across the genome, along with a bespoke genotyping array designed for this species. Their modelling suggests that using the results to choose breeding pairs could lengthen average survival after exposure by around 9% in the next generation.

Crucially, the approach appears able to do this while keeping inbreeding under control and preserving genetic diversity. For very small populations, that is rather good news, since diversity is central to long-term health and the ability to adapt.

The project was a joint effort between two universities, the New South Wales government and two zoo organisations, Zoos Victoria and Taronga Conservation Society. The zoos' existing breeding programme provided a natural testing ground. The work also draws on six years spent mapping the frog's genome and building up disease-resilience records.

Professor Lee Skerratt, the senior author, believes the method could suit other threatened wildlife battling persistent disease, especially species already bred under human care. Hundreds of amphibian species are now held in captivity for conservation, with chytrid still a threat to populations worldwide.

This remains early-stage research, and the 9% figure is a prediction rather than a result. The team plans to refine it using genetic data from more of the breeding population and to check whether the gains appear in later generations. The biggest question is whether better survival under laboratory conditions leads to better outcomes in the wild.

## Why it matters

Keeping a species alive in captivity is no mean feat, but it only buys time if the threat that brought it to the brink is still out there. This work points towards breeding programmes that prepare animals for life alongside a disease, rather than simply holding them in reserve. If the approach holds up, it could give conservationists a new tool for many other amphibians facing chytrid.
