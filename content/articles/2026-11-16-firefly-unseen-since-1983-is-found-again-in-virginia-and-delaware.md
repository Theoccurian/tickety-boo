A firefly that nobody had documented since 1983 has reappeared, and not just once. The similar firefly (Pyractomena similis) was seen twice in Virginia and once in Delaware this spring, a trio of sightings reported in the Journal of Lost Species.

The first came on 15 May, when an observer watched males flashing above a Virginia meadow and uploaded the sighting to iNaturalist, an online platform for citizen scientists. Two weeks later, a second group of the insects was found close by.

The Delaware discovery followed on a June night in Brandywine Creek State Park. Entomologist Christopher Heckscher, of Delaware State University, was standing in a meadow with his camera, waiting for the fireflies to emerge. A quick run of amber pulses stood out from the rest, and he says he was sure of the species the moment he saw the rhythm. It was the first time the insect had been recorded in the state.

The meadow was a tangle of low vegetation, and the species is known to favour open ground near water. Heckscher notes that such habitat is scarcer than people assume, because fields are so often grazed or mown.

Past records of the firefly run from Maryland to Mississippi, yet it has never been studied closely. "There's hardly any information out there about it," Heckscher said.

It is far from the only firefly to have vanished from the record. The same paper names 28 species in the US and Canada as lost to science, meaning no confirmed sighting for at least ten years. That amounts to 16% of the region's known native fireflies.

The authors, though, sound a hopeful note. They think many of these insects are overlooked rather than extinct, and nine species from a list drawn up in 2024 have already been found again. Some turned up in targeted surveys, others thanks to people enjoying the outdoors and noting what they saw, and experts combing through iNaturalist posts spotted several. A cracking result for patient observers.

## Why it matters

Fireflies face habitat loss, pesticides, light pollution and invasive species, and for many kinds there is too little information to judge how they are faring. Rediscoveries such as this show that well-aimed searches and keen amateur naturalists can help fill those gaps. Looking after the meadows, clean water and dark nights that fireflies need also benefits other insects, many of which are in decline worldwide.
