Pines and other conifers taken from the Northern Hemisphere and planted in the Southern Hemisphere can race ahead of their relatives back home, growing as much as four times as quickly. They also stand up better to drought, according to a large international study published in Nature.

More than two dozen researchers took part, drawing on 192 sites across four continents. They measured growth in pines, spruces, firs, cedars and other conifers, both in their native ranges and in places far from them.

The finding runs against expectations. Ecological theory says plants should face a trade-off between growing fast and coping with harsh conditions, yet these trees seem to have avoided it, which is no mean feat. Stephen Brewer, an ecologist and biology professor at the University of Mississippi, called the result surprising.

It matters because many governments and groups want to plant trees where none grew before, using them as carbon sinks. Brewer pointed out that such schemes work best when the trees grow quickly and do not die in dry spells.

The reason is not yet pinned down. The researchers did find, though, that the transplanted conifers held more carbohydrates, including sugars and starches, which can serve as an energy store through difficult seasons. Alex Fajardo, a professor at the University of Talca in Chile, said identifying this likely mechanism was a valuable part of the work.

The team was quick to add some caution. Pines and Douglas firs have been grown for timber in Chile, Brazil and South Africa for decades, and Fajardo warned that nonnative plantations have brought high biodiversity and social costs. Brewer described pines escaping into Brazil's Cerrado savanna, where they grow even faster and crowd out native plants.

Fire is another worry. Brewer noted that savannas depend on fire, and that densely planted slash pines could raise the risk of it. Mississippi has seen something similar: foresters there swapped longleaf pines for faster-growing slash pines from the 1930s, and many are now returning to the more fire-resistant longleaf.

There may be a better option. Southern beeches, native Southern Hemisphere trees of the genus Nothofagus, also grew and tolerated drought reasonably well. "They may be a more potentially sustainable alternative," Fajardo said.

## Why it matters

Tree planting is one of the best-known ways of drawing carbon dioxide out of the air, but the wrong species in the wrong place can damage wildlife and local communities. This study gives planners a clearer idea of how trees behave far from home, along with a plain warning about the risks. It also points to native species that might do much of the same work with fewer drawbacks.
