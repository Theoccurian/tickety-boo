Karl Bushby has walked home to Hull after nearly three decades on the road, finishing his round-the-world trek with a tearful embrace at his mother's front gate.

The former paratrooper, now 57, set off in 1998 with one firm rule: he would return home entirely on foot. He has since covered more than 31,000 miles through 30 countries, pushing a cart loaded with medicine, sleeping bags and a spare tyre. Local well-wishers accompanied him for the last three miles.

On 2 November, local officials and hundreds of supporters welcomed him in Queen Victoria Square. He then met his mother, Angela, who was in tears, and the pair looked at one another properly for the first time in almost 30 years.

The route was far from smooth. Bushby crossed the Darien Gap, a tract of thick rainforest and mountains in South America, and was arrested in Russia. He had originally aimed to finish in 2012, but arrests and visa troubles forced him to pause for long spells.

His own rules ban any mechanised transport, ferries included, so stretches of water had to be swum. The year before, he swam over 170 miles of the Caspian Sea, from Kazakhstan to Azerbaijan, to steer clear of Iran and Russia. It took him a month.

Near the end, the operators of the Channel Tunnel turned down his request to use its service tunnel. Bushby therefore swam the English Channel, putting in 20 hours across three days. He rested on a safety vessel at night, went back to his last position each morning, and finally came ashore at Shakespeare Beach near Dover.

He is recognised for the longest unbroken journey on foot in history, beating the Earth's circumference by roughly 6,000 miles. His list of firsts also includes a 2006 trip with fellow adventurer Dimitri Kieffer over the frozen Bering Strait, from Alaska into Russia, partly by swimming between ice floes.

Bushby was quick to thank those who had helped. "It took a world to get me here," he said, pointing to the kindness of strangers, individuals and nations alike.

## Why it matters

Sticking with a single goal for 28 years is no mean feat, especially when delays and refusals repeatedly pushed the finish line further away. Bushby's own account puts the generosity of strangers at the heart of the story, a welcome reminder that big undertakings are rarely done alone. It also shows that a long-held ambition can still be completed after many setbacks.
