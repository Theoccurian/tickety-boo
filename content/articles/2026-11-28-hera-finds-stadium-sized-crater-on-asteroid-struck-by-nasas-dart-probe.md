Europe's Hera spacecraft has sent back the first images of Dimorphos, the small asteroid that NASA deliberately struck in 2022, and they hold a few surprises. The side hit by the DART probe has been flattened into a crater about the size of a sports stadium, and the whole asteroid is now tumbling erratically as it circles its larger companion, Didymos.

Dimorphos measures 151 metres across, while Didymos is 730 metres wide. The pair are currently 193.5 million km from Earth. When DART struck on 26 September 2022, it shortened Dimorphos's orbit by 33 minutes, far more than had been predicted. Telescopes on Earth could time that change, but nobody could say what had happened to the asteroid itself.

Scientists had argued over whether the impact had dug a crater, altered the shape or left the body unchanged. According to Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel, the answer is all three. The crater runs across the asteroid's full width, yet the boulder fields on its other faces look just as they did in DART's own 2022 pictures.

Hera has also spotted hundreds of previously unknown boulders, each only a few metres across, circling the pair. The team will have to take them into account as the spacecraft edges closer in the coming weeks.

The craft, about the size of a small car, is currently watching from around 25 to 30 km away. It carries three instruments, including a camera, a 25-colour imager and a Japanese heat-sensing device that measures surface temperatures. In December it is due to move in to roughly 13 km for sharper pictures.

In January 2027, Hera plans to release two shoebox-sized CubeSats, a plucky pair that will go closer still. Juventas, led by Luxembourg, will use radar to peer inside an asteroid for the first time and may attempt a landing. Italian-led Milani will study asteroid material and dust.

ESA Director-General Josef Aschbacher said: "This is international collaboration at its best". Japan's space agency, JAXA, is among the partners that contributed.

## Why it matters

An asteroid impact is a rare but serious threat to life on Earth, and DART showed that a spacecraft can nudge an asteroid onto a different path. Knowing what the collision did to the asteroid's surface, shape and motion helps scientists judge how reliably the technique could be used. Hera's findings turn a one-off experiment into evidence that future planetary defence plans can be built on.
