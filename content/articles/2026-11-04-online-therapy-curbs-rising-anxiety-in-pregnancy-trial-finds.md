A short online programme that pregnant women can do at home has cut anxiety both before and after birth, a King's College London trial has found. The programme, called RELAX (REducing Levels of AnXiety), was compared with usual care, and the results appear in JAMA Network Open.

The trial recruited 268 women who were 16 to 28 weeks pregnant. All had a strong habit of repetitive negative thinking and anxiety of up to moderate level, but none had a psychiatric diagnosis. Half received RELAX alongside usual care, while the rest had usual care only.

RELAX also brought lower levels of depression before and after birth. It reduced repetitive negative thinking as well, which means dwelling on the worst possible outcome of a situation. That habit is central to anxiety, and it is exactly what the programme targets.

Participants in the therapy group worked through 12 short sessions on a laptop or tablet, spread across a month. In each one they met 30 scenarios that could be read in a positive or a negative way, some linked to pregnancy and others to everyday life. The idea was to coach them towards kinder, more balanced interpretations.

Take-up was strong. Of those offered RELAX, 81.3% finished at least 10 of the 12 sessions, and most said they would recommend it. One participant said she was coping much better with her worries, and had felt excited about her 20-week scan, which had previously been a real trigger for her.

Earlier work had already shown the approach can help people with high anxiety. This is the first time it has been tested as a way of stopping low levels of anxiety from growing, which is no mean feat.

Following feedback from users and stakeholders, the team is turning RELAX into an app named LENS4Pregnancy. They are working with people who have lived experience, clinicians, policymakers, charities and health providers to draw up plans for introducing it.

## Why it matters

Anxiety disorders affect around one in five pregnant people, and the source links anxiety in the perinatal period to a greater chance of preterm birth and lower birth weight. Women without a diagnosis often miss out on mental health support. A therapy used at home could reach many of them while their worries are still manageable, though the app is not yet available and plans for its rollout are still being developed.
