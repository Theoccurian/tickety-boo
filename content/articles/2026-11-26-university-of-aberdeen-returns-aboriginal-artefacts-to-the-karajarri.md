Shields, digging sticks and sacred men's objects that left Western Australia almost 100 years ago have been handed back to the Karajarri people by the University of Aberdeen.

The anthropologist Dr Ralph Piddington collected the items in 1930 and 1931, during fieldwork carried out while he was a student at the University of Sydney. They later made their way to Scotland, where they remained until now.

The university said it had seen no evidence that the objects were obtained unethically. Even so, both sides agreed that they belong with the Karajarri community, for whom they are described as a key part of their culture.

The return was announced at a ceremony attended by Karajarri representatives and Australian government officials. It marked the end of three years of work between the university, Karajarri elders and the cultural heritage programme at AIATSIS, the Australian body devoted to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander studies.

Martin Bin Rashid, chief executive of the Karajarri Traditional Lands Association, said the community was welcoming home an important collection that had been away from its country and people for almost a century. "These objects are much more than museum items," he said. He added that they carry the history, identity, knowledge and cultural connections of the community's ancestors.

Neil Curtis, the university's head of collections, said the ceremony was about more than the objects themselves. In his view, addressing the legacies of museum collections can deepen cultural understanding and help people and communities revive cultural knowledge and practice. He called it an honour to return the items to the community they came from.

This is rather good news for the Karajarri, and it is not the first time Aberdeen has taken such a step. In 2021 the university returned a Benin Bronze to Nigeria, and in 2025 it sent Tasmanian Aboriginal ancestral remains home.

## Why it matters

Objects of cultural and spiritual importance carry the most meaning in the community that created and values them. Returning them to the Karajarri places them back in the hands of the people to whom they matter most. As the university itself points out, such returns can also help communities revive cultural knowledge and practice.
