A once-daily tablet has lowered the chance of early-stage breast cancer coming back, according to a large international trial co-led by the UCLA Health Jonsson Comprehensive Cancer Center. People given the investigational drug giredestrant were 30% less likely, in relative terms, to suffer an invasive recurrence or to die than those on standard hormone therapy. The results are published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

The phase 3 trial, called lidERA, involved 4,170 patients with stage 1, 2 or 3 hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. All had undergone surgery and faced an intermediate or high risk of recurrence, and some had also had chemotherapy.

Patients were assigned at random to giredestrant (2,084 people) or to tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor (2,086 people). Treatment was planned for five years in both groups, and follow-up had reached a median of about 32 months.

Giredestrant also cut the relative risk of the disease turning up at distant sites, including the lungs, liver and bones, by 31%. Cancer that has spread in this way is harder to treat.

Side effects were broadly alike in the two groups, with joint pain the most common complaint. Fewer patients on giredestrant gave up treatment because of them, which is a cracking result for a medicine meant to be taken for years.

The drug belongs to a newer class known as selective estrogen receptor degraders. It stops estrogen attaching to receptors on cancer cells and also encourages those receptors to be broken down, interrupting the signals that help the cells grow. That two-pronged approach may help get round resistance to older treatments.

Dr Aditya Bardia, the trial's global principal investigator, described the findings as a significant step forward. He pointed out that this is the first potential new hormone therapy option for early-stage, estrogen receptor-positive disease in more than 20 years.

The team will keep following participants to see whether the benefit lasts and whether overall survival improves. Other studies are testing giredestrant alongside additional targeted therapies. The US Food and Drug Administration is reviewing the drug, with a decision expected by 30 November 2026.

## Why it matters

About 70% of breast cancers are hormone receptor-positive, so even a modest gain in preventing recurrence could reach a great many people. Existing treatments have improved outcomes, but some cancers still return or become resistant, and side effects can make it hard to keep going. A pill that works better and is at least as easy to tolerate could help more patients finish their course. The drug remains investigational, and longer follow-up will show whether the benefit endures.
