A total of 3,400 one-flowered wintergreen plants have been moved to safety at Culbin Forest in Moray, after the shoreline where they grew began to fall into the sea. The tiny, star-shaped wildflower is among the rarest in Britain, and its UK population is estimated to have shrunk by more than half over the past 50 years.

Plantlife Scotland worked with Forestry and Land Scotland, NatureScot and 18 volunteers to carry out the move. Between two and three metres of land had collapsed into the sea in recent years. The rescue followed a smaller trial and received support from the Scottish government's nature restoration fund.

Moving the plants was no mean feat. One-flowered wintergreen relies on a web of fungi in the soil, so volunteers cut out whole sections of earth around the plants rather than lifting them bare, keeping those underground networks intact. The species makes energy through photosynthesis, but it can also draw it from woodland fungi, which has made its decline hard to untangle.

In the UK, the plant grows only in pine woods in north-east Scotland, and survives at just seven sites. Only three of those hold healthy populations. Plantlife says changes in forest management have altered the conditions the fungi need, while hotter, drier weather linked to the climate crisis has reduced the moisture in the soil.

About 50,000 plants are thought to remain in Scotland, but that figure rather flatters the species. The plants spread through their roots, producing growth that is genetically identical, so there may be fewer than 500 genetically distinct individuals. At one site, roughly 100 plants amounted to just two of them.

Iain Macdonald, a biodiversity adviser at NatureScot, said it is not yet known how many plants will survive at their new home, but that leaving them would only have delayed their loss. "Any survival will be a success," he said.

A small number of plants and seeds have also been taken to another wood where the species has not been recorded before, in the hope of establishing a new population.

## Why it matters

A plant that survives at only seven UK sites, with just three in good health, has little room for further losses. Moving it away from a coastline that is wearing away gives these plants a chance they would not otherwise have had. Macdonald also said that adding plants to small existing populations could strengthen their ability to cope with a changing environment, which makes this a welcome boost for a flower that has seen its numbers fall.
