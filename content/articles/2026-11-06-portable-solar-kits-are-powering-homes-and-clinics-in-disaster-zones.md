Portable solar kits are becoming a regular feature of humanitarian relief, supplying electricity to homes, clinics and water filters where the grid has been knocked out or never existed. Aid groups, it seems, increasingly see them as a real necessity rather than a trial run.

In the Rohingya camp at Teknaf, Bangladesh, Mohammed Saber, 35, carries a single solar panel up to the roof of his family's bamboo-and-tarpaulin home each morning. His family fled the Myanmar military's crackdown in 2017, and he has kept up the routine for six years. That one panel charges their phones, lets them cook by light, runs fans in the hot season and gives the children light to study by after dark.

The label "solar kit" covers a good deal. Sarah Rosenberg-Jansen, a researcher at the Stockholm Environment Institute, says it can mean anything from lanterns, torches and heating pads to complete home systems and electric cook stoves. They share one great virtue: they are portable and need no grid, so they can be put to work straight away in an emergency.

Examples are scattered across the globe. A tuberculosis clinic in Karachi run by Doctors Without Borders is solar-powered. In Renk County, South Sudan, the UN migration agency has fitted solar-driven water filters for families who fled the war in neighbouring Sudan. After floods in Nepal in August killed 1,455 people and left 5,285 missing, kits were handed out to survivors cut off from the grid.

The nonprofit Hope For Ukraine has also supplied kits to thousands of families, among them people aged around 75 to 80. Chief executive Yuriy Boyechko says: "It's just a matter of plug and play."

Price has played its part. Fossil fuels are costly and difficult to haul into disaster areas, whereas solar panels avoid that price volatility. Solar was a luxury a few decades ago, but mass manufacturing and steady gains in efficiency have brought kits within reach of aid budgets.

Obstacles remain. Many agencies hold long-standing contracts built around diesel generators, and moving to solar means trusting new equipment and setting up fresh supply chains, sometimes in dangerous conditions. Aimee Jenks of the Global Platform for Action says cuts to donor funding have stripped out much of the sector's energy expertise.

Hope For Ukraine expects to have distributed more than 3,200 kits by the end of 2026, no mean feat given that funding meant the figure took two years to reach. About 20,000 families are waiting.

## Why it matters

Power in a crisis is about far more than a light bulb: it keeps phones charged, medical care going, water cleaner and children learning. Solar kits do not rely on fuel deliveries or a working grid, which makes them well suited to places that are hardest to reach. The long waiting list shows how much demand there is, and how far future funding will shape the number of families who benefit.
