Discarded plumbing pipes, window frames and out-of-date credit cards could one day find a second career inside engine oil. Chemists at Virginia Tech have devised a way to turn polyvinyl chloride (PVC), among the most awkward plastics to recycle, into polyalphaolefin, a base oil for synthetic lubricants. The study appears in the journal Nature.

Chlorine is at the root of the trouble, along with the assortment of additives that manufacturers blend in to make PVC bendy, stiff or heat-resistant. Those additives confound ordinary recycling methods, so a large share of the world's used PVC is buried in landfill.

The new method, led by chemist and chemical engineer Guoliang "Greg" Liu, begins by dissolving PVC in a solvent. Aluminium trichloride is then added, along with alpha-olefins, hydrocarbons that are already staples of synthetic lubricant manufacture. After three hours at 158°F, the team can draw off a fairly thick oil.

The idea did not arrive smoothly. At first, the researchers tried to replace the chlorine atoms along PVC's long molecular chains with other chemical groups, but they kept ending up with a soft, sticky goo. Liu then asked whether it might be better to snap the chains into shorter segments, and that change of approach paid off.

Eric Munyaneza Nuwayo, then in the last year of his doctorate, led the project, with chemistry graduate student Connor S. Thompson and first-year student Abby Civiello alongside him. Ali Erdemir at Texas A&M University assessed how the oil performed, William Goddard at Caltech provided chemical computations, and Xi Chen at Virginia Tech built models of how it might be made on a bigger scale.

Taking the process out of the laboratory is the next challenge, and no mean feat. Liu also wants to make the technology greener still, and the resulting oils more widely available. "Lubricants are the silent hero out there," he said.

## Why it matters

Plastic pollution is a stubborn problem, and PVC is one of the hardest types to deal with, so any practical use for it is rather good news. Lubricants matter too: they cut friction and wear in everything from lawn mowers to jet engines, and demand for them keeps growing. A route that turns waste into a valuable material tackles both issues at once, although it must still be proved beyond the laboratory.
