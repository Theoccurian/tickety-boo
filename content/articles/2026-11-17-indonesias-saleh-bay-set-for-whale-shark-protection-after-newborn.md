The governor of West Nusa Tenggara has formally designated 73,165 hectares of Indonesia's Saleh Bay for whale shark conservation. The move extends an existing marine protected area across a big share of a bay that totals 145,900 hectares. Indonesia's Ministry of Marine Affairs and Fisheries is now reviewing the proposals, and the nonprofit Konservasi Indonesia hopes for formal recognition in 2027.

The bay lies in the shadow of Mount Tambora on Sumbawa Island. Adult whale sharks pass through it and feed there, and there is growing evidence that juveniles shelter in its food-rich waters, free of predators. The sharks have been monitored since 2015, and the latest population assessment, published in July, counted 174 individuals across 884 sightings. Researchers tell them apart by their unique spot patterns.

The assessment also recorded something new. Since August 2024, fishers have reported five sightings of very small whale sharks, measuring between 1.2 and 1.5 metres. A video of a similar-sized shark caught in nets a month later confirmed that these youngsters really were whale sharks. Only 33 sightings of whale sharks under 1.5 metres appear in earlier scientific papers, according to Konservasi Indonesia's Mochamad Iqbal Herwata Putra.

Researchers are careful not to run ahead of the evidence. Births within the bay have not been confirmed, so the case for calling it a nursery remains preliminary.

The fishers deserve much of the credit. They work from bagans, vessels that use nets and lights to catch shrimp and squid at night, and the lights draw in whale sharks too. That gives the crews a view of the animals' lives that conventional surveys could easily miss.

Attitudes have changed. The sharks eat schooling fish such as anchovies and sardines, so fishers once saw them as a threat. Now the main nuisance is a damaged net when a shark gets caught, and Konservasi Indonesia is training fishers as guides who take tourists out to see the sharks responsibly. Adam, a fisher, says incomes have risen quite a lot and that people who had no work now have some, a welcome boost for the area.

A draft management plan has been discussed with government and community representatives. "It's not just about putting lines over a map," said Meizani Irmadhiany of Konservasi Indonesia, stressing that fishing communities, the wider ecosystem and activities such as diving must all be considered.

## Why it matters

Whale sharks are classed as endangered, and Indo-Pacific numbers have fallen by 63% over the past 75 years, with targeted fishing, bycatch and vessel strikes among the threats. The species can live for around 80 years and does not mature until about 30, so safeguarding the places where young sharks grow up is especially valuable. Protection that draws on the knowledge of local fishers also gives the plan a practical footing in the community.
