A daily pill taken alongside chemotherapy has stopped tumours growing in 90% of people in an early trial for newly diagnosed, advanced pancreatic cancer. Cancer Research UK has described the results as "the biggest leap forward in decades".

The drug is called daraxonrasib. It was tested on 61 patients whose cancer had already spread elsewhere in the body. A little over six in ten saw their tumours get smaller or vanish, and in around one in 12 patients, about 8%, the disease could no longer be seen on CT scans.

Doctors are careful not to overstate things. Participants were monitored for roughly eighteen months on average, and for some the cancer had not come back by the end of that period. Even so, longer studies are needed to establish whether remission is possible and how long it might last.

Dr Sam Godfrey of Cancer Research UK said progress against this disease was long overdue. He also pointed out that a clear scan does not guarantee someone is free of cancer until more time has passed.

Dr Wungki Park, of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in the US, is involved in the trial. He highlighted deeper responses and disease control lasting nearly twice as long, which he said might eventually mean longer survival.

Daraxonrasib is a KRAS inhibitor and is already approved in the US. It attaches to RAS mutations, present in most pancreatic cancers, and switches them off. Those mutations help fuel tumour growth. Similar mutations turn up in dozens of other cancers, lung and ovarian among them, so the drug could in time benefit many more people.

The findings are being shared with doctors at a European cancer conference run by EORTC.

## Why it matters

Among common cancers in the UK, pancreatic cancer carries the poorest chance of survival, with around 11,500 diagnoses and 10,200 deaths a year in Britain, according to Cancer Research UK. Chemotherapy can shrink tumours somewhat, but they often return, and most people with advanced disease live for months rather than years. A treatment that holds the cancer back for longer would be rather good news, although larger and longer trials must confirm the benefit.
