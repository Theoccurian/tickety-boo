A single iron drip helped people with the most frequently seen form of heart failure perform better on fitness tests, according to a randomised trial from the Mass General Brigham Heart and Vascular Institute. All of the patients had heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and were also short of iron.

The IRON-MET HFpEF trial recruited 65 people with symptoms of the condition. Their median age was 68, and 72% were women. Forty-four received one 1,000-mg intravenous dose of ferric derisomaltose, while the other 21 were given a placebo. Iron deficiency is found in more than half of all patients with HFpEF.

The team chose a drip rather than tablets because earlier studies in other types of heart failure showed no benefit from iron taken by mouth, probably because the gut absorbs it poorly.

Every participant had a cardiopulmonary exercise test before the infusion and another 12 weeks afterwards. Peak oxygen uptake, a key indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness, rose by about 10% in the iron group. In the placebo group it did not change.

Several other measures also favoured the iron group. Iron availability in the blood improved, and so did six-minute walking distance, patients' New York Heart Association class and oxygen uptake at the anaerobic threshold. No serious adverse events were linked to the iron.

There was one caveat. Scores from a detailed questionnaire on how patients felt about their own health did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Lead author Gregory D. Lewis said this was the first study to look comprehensively at how exercise responds when iron is replaced in patients with HFpEF and iron deficiency. Senior author Rajeev Malhotra called the gain in peak oxygen uptake "a clinically meaningful increment for patients with heart failure".

The results were presented at the American Heart Association's 2026 Scientific Sessions and published at the same time in the journal Circulation.

The authors are open about the limits of their work. The group was small and mostly white, and patients were followed for only 12 weeks. They want further studies to establish whether the benefits hold in more diverse populations and last over longer periods.

## Why it matters

Lewis described patients with HFpEF as burdened by severe limits on how much exercise they can tolerate, so a measurable gain in fitness is rather good news. Because iron deficiency is so common in this group, the findings suggest that topping up iron could help tackle those physical limits. The researchers say the next question is whether intravenous iron can also improve longer-term clinical outcomes.
