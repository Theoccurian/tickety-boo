Scientists have built the first cell-by-cell map covering the entire body of an adult vertebrate, and they did it with a fish about a centimetre long. The work, from the Whitehead Institute, is published in the journal Science.

The subject is Danionella cerebrum, a transparent species whose brain can be seen from above through the top of its head. The team analysed more than 600,000 individual cells to record which genes are active in each one and where those cells sit. Such a record is called a single-cell transcriptome atlas: a snapshot of the RNA molecules a cell has made from its DNA.

Size has been the sticking point for projects like this. Doing it for a larger animal is technically demanding and expensive. Danionella's body, about 0.4 inches long, brought the job within reach, and its close kinship with zebrafish lets researchers borrow genetic tools already developed for that well-known laboratory species.

Cells were gathered from the nervous system, eyes, organs, fins and beyond. The team then used spatial techniques to place cell types and gene activity back in their positions within tissue. The result was detailed enough to pick up exceptionally rare cell populations.

Some intriguing patterns emerged. Neurons from the same region of the nervous system tended to resemble one another in gene activity, even when they relied on different chemical messengers. Fibroblasts, a kind of connective tissue cell, also held on to region-specific activity in genes best known for shaping the body during development. In the fins, for instance, cells kept programmes linked to forelimb and hindlimb development in other vertebrates.

What those lingering signals do in adult fish is not yet known, though they may help tissues keep or rebuild the right structures.

The atlas also proved its worth as a reference for regeneration. After the researchers injured the telencephalon, part of the forebrain, the missing neurons grew back. The team traced the whole sequence, from the early wound and immune response to stem-cell activity and the maturing of new neurons.

The idea grew from Kutay Deniz Atabay's interest in watching nervous systems regenerate and from Peter Reddien's lab experience with a similar atlas for flatworms. Reddien recalled thinking that a complete vertebrate atlas might be possible. "Here's an animal where we'd have a chance," he said.

The atlas is freely available through DigiFish, an interactive online resource, so other researchers can explore it themselves.

## Why it matters

Knowing an organism's list of genes is one thing; knowing how each type of cell uses them is quite another. A whole-body atlas gives scientists a baseline for comparing the effects of injury, ageing or drugs across an entire animal, rather than one tissue at a time. Because the fish is transparent, closely related to a popular lab species and able to regenerate, it is a cracking candidate for studying how repair works and how different parts of the body influence one another.
