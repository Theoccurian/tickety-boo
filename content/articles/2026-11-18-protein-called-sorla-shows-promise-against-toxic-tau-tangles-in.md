A protein the brain already makes may prove a useful ally against Alzheimer's disease. Researchers at Sanford Burnham Prebys found that raising levels of SORLA reduced the build-up of toxic tau tangles and helped keep connections between nerve cells intact. The work is early laboratory research and has not yet been tested in people.

Tau normally does a valuable job, steadying the tiny internal scaffolding that supports neurons. In Alzheimer's and related conditions it turns abnormal and sticks together in clumps, which are linked to cognitive decline and the death of nerve cells. The findings are published in Science Advances.

SORLA was already known to limit amyloid-beta, the other great hallmark of Alzheimer's. Whether it also kept tau in check was largely unknown. The team found that extra SORLA dampened an abnormal chemical change to tau and weakened the ability of misfolded tau to "seed" further clumping.

It also safeguarded synapses, the junctions where neurons pass messages, along with the flexibility of those links that learning and memory depend upon. Less brain shrinkage was seen too, which is rather encouraging. Lead author Huijie Huang described the reduction in shrinkage and tau as very exciting to see.

The opposite held when the protein was absent. Tau-related damage grew more severe, underlining how protective SORLA may be. Some people carry changes in the gene that produces it, so the work may eventually help explain why some are hit harder than others.

Close analysis of individual cells also produced a promising lead. Without SORLA, activity rose in a family of signalling receptors known as plexin-B, and medicines that target this family already exist. The researchers suggest such drugs might one day be repurposed to calm overactive support cells in the brain, known as glia.

Much remains to be done. The team plans to look at how different types of brain cell respond as SORLA levels rise and fall. They also want to study human cells in a diseased setting, since human and laboratory cells are not the same.

## Why it matters

Alzheimer's and related dementias involve more than one kind of damage, so approaches that tackle both amyloid and tau are widely sought. Showing that one natural protein can influence tau as well as amyloid gives researchers a fresh target to explore. The fact that drugs aimed at plexin-B receptors already exist could also make it quicker to test new ideas, though plenty of work lies ahead.
