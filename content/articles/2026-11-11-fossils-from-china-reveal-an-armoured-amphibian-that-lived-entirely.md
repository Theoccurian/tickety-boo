A bony-plated amphibian that seems to have lived its entire life in the sea has been identified from fossils found in China's Guizhou Province. Named Gerrothorax maritimus, it swam in an ancient ocean nearly 237 million years ago, in the Late Triassic. Nothing alive among today's amphibians is known to live that way.

The evidence comes from two exceptionally well-preserved skeletons. An international team studied them, drawing on scientists at Peking University and the Geological Museum of Guizhou, working with colleagues at the University of California, Davis. Their results are published in Nature Ecology & Evolution. When the animals were alive, the area lay beneath an ocean hundreds of miles from any major landmass.

The creature was nearly three feet long, and its shape was nothing like a modern salamander's. It was broad, flattened and disc-like, with thick bony armour that may have shielded it from big marine predators such as ichthyosaurs. In body mass it was probably similar to a giant salamander, though a good deal chunkier.

Its skull and armour point to an ambush hunter. The researchers think it lay on the seafloor and sucked in prey that strayed within reach, much as the wobbegong, a carpet shark, does today.

Salt water is a real obstacle for amphibians. Among the more than 8,000 living species, fewer than 2% can tolerate any salt, and none is known to spend its whole life at sea. Their skin is permeable and is used for breathing and taking in water, which leaves them exposed in a salty environment.

The team proposes that the armour limited how much water passed through this ancient animal's skin. It may instead have taken in seawater through its mouth and absorbed it via the gut, while gills, known in other members of the genus, got rid of surplus salt. This explanation is a suggestion rather than something proved.

Several clues support a marine life. The skeletons lay in deep marine sediments, and their fingers were still preserved, which suggests the animals died close to where they lived. Strontium isotopes in the fossils also matched those expected in Late Triassic seawater, no mean feat of detective work.

## Why it matters

Mammals, reptiles and birds each include species that live entirely at sea, yet scientists had never found convincing fossil evidence of an amphibian doing the same. Gerrothorax maritimus appears to have overcome the limits that keep its living relatives tied to fresh water, although how it did so remains a proposal. The discovery adds an unexpected chapter to the story of amphibian evolution.
