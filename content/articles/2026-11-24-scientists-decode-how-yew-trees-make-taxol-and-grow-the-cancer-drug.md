Scientists have worked out the complete natural recipe for Taxol, one of the world's most important cancer drugs, and have got tobacco plants to make it. The work, published in Nature Chemical Biology, could eventually help to lower the price of the medicine.

Taxol, also called paclitaxel, treats breast, ovarian and lung cancers. It originates in the yew tree, which makes the active compound in its bark. Today's manufacturing starts by extracting related plant compounds and then converting them into the drug through a long sequence of intricate chemistry, which is why Taxol ranks among the costliest medicines around.

Although the drug has been in use for a long time, nobody had a full account of how the plant builds it. A team led by Professor Sotirios Kampranis, of the Department of Plant and Environmental Sciences, tested numerous ideas to piece together the whole pathway from beginning to end.

To check their work, the researchers took candidate genes from the yew and placed them in Nicotiana benthamiana, a quick-growing relative of the tobacco plant that laboratories commonly use. According to postdoctoral researcher Zhen Fan, a co-first author, more than 20 yew genes went in, so the team could show each stage of the process in turn. The tobacco plants made Taxol in amounts similar to those in yew bark, a cracking result.

Kampranis says tobacco suits the job because it offers the same cellular surroundings as the yew, yet grows much faster and is simpler to engineer.

There is still some way to go. For large-scale production, the yew genes would have to become a permanent part of the tobacco plant's own genetic makeup, so they need not be added again each time. "That is the next major challenge," Kampranis says.

Tobacco is not the only option. The team also considers fermented yeast cells a promising alternative, and other researchers are now free to build on the blueprint with whichever method they prefer.

## Why it matters

Cancer diagnoses are expected to keep climbing around the world, and the price of established drugs can put them out of reach for patients in poorer countries. Kampranis points out that Danish hospitals rely less on cheap Taxol, but that affordable supplies matter enormously elsewhere. A published blueprint gives scientists everywhere a firm starting point for devising simpler and cheaper ways to make the drug, though those methods have yet to be scaled up.
