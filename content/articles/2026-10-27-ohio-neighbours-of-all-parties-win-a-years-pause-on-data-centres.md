Grove City council in Ohio has passed a one-year moratorium on data centre development, following a campaign in which Republicans, Democrats and Independents worked together. The vote came in June, after residents learned that a developer wanted to build a large facility on their doorstep.

The company, Texas-based Headwaters, hoped to annex land in Pleasant Township and Jackson Township for a site of more than 300 acres in Grove City. Both townships had already passed their own moratoriums in the first half of 2026.

A group called Protect Grove City, run by four people, did much of the groundwork. "We got organized," says Jen Belt, one of those behind it. Volunteers taped flyers to every home within three miles of the site, encouraged neighbours to attend council meetings and gathered more than 4,000 signatures on an online petition. Getting a city council to act on the back of that is no mean feat.

Belt, a registered Democrat, and Patty Spillman, a lifelong Republican who farms more than 400 acres across the road from the proposed site, are firmly on the same side. Neither lives within Grove City proper, so neither can vote on the council's decisions, but both consider it home. Belt says volunteers deliberately steer clear of divisive politics, even though some hold very different views from hers.

The group knows a moratorium only buys time. So it has secured a place on the November ballot for a Community Consent Amendment. If voters pass it, residents' approval would be required before any large-scale development could break ground. It is aimed at data centres but is written to cover all heavy industry.

Their worries are chiefly practical: energy costs, water, noise and property values. Belt is especially concerned about the power infrastructure that might follow, namely natural gas plants. Under a 2025 Ohio law, qualifying power plants built to serve data centres no longer need local approval and instead go through an accelerated state review.

Grove City is far from alone. A University of Massachusetts Amherst poll found very few people want an A.I. data centre in their community. When opponents were asked why, only 13 per cent cited rejection of artificial intelligence or distrust of the industry, while far more pointed to environmental concerns, rising utility bills and land use.

## Why it matters

Data centres use large amounts of electricity and water, and the communities that host them often feel the effects first. Grove City shows that neighbours with different politics can find common ground over local concerns, and that organised, good-natured persistence can move a council. Whether voters will back the ballot measure is not yet known, but the moratorium gives residents a year to make their case.
