Researchers at McGill University have designed a single, joined-up workflow that takes a child with a rare neurodevelopmental disorder from genetic diagnosis to a therapy built and tested around their own condition. The work is published in the American Journal of Human Genetics.

The problem it tackles is a stubborn one. When the genetic change behind a child's condition affects only a few people, there is rarely a realistic route to treatment. The authors argue that a diagnosis ought to mark the start of assessing possible therapies, not the point at which the journey stops.

Their pipeline starts with a sample of the patient's blood. From this, the team produces brain cells that carry the same genetic change as the child. Those cells give scientists something to study the disorder in, and a way to try out possible treatments in the laboratory.

As a worked example, the researchers used the method to develop a trial therapy based on RNA, aimed at one child with Kleefstra syndrome. This rare genetic condition can slow development and bring intellectual disability, along with trouble speaking and communicating. The treatment was tested only in laboratory cells, so this is early-stage research, and more work is needed before it could be tried in people.

Lead author Julia Macintosh, a PhD student in McGill's Department of Neurology and Neurosurgery, said the idea is to use a diagnosis to steer the design of a genetic therapy. Senior author Carl Ernst, who leads the Interventional Genetics Unit at the Neuro, described the project as a fine example of McGill and CHU Sainte-Justine pulling together for families in Quebec. He hopes diagnostic centres elsewhere will find the model useful too.

Rather encouragingly, the authors point out that many of the technologies needed for personalised genetic therapies already exist. The remaining task is to fit them into health care systems so that more patients can benefit.

## Why it matters

For families of children with very rare conditions, a diagnosis can explain what is happening without pointing to anything that can be done about it. Because each condition may affect so few people, developing treatments one by one has been hard to justify. A repeatable pathway that turns an individual diagnosis into a testable therapy could, in time, narrow that gap, although this work is still at an early stage.
