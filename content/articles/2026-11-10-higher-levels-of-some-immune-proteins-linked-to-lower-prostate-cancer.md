Men who carry higher levels of certain immune proteins in their blood appear less likely to go on to develop prostate cancer, according to a large study by Oxford Population Health. The research, published in Nature Communications, suggests the body's defences may be keeping rogue cells in check long before any tumour can be found.

Prostate cancer is the most common cancer among men in the UK. The recognised risk factors are age, ethnicity, family history and genetics, none of which a person can alter, and there are no proven ways to prevent the disease.

The team looked at 368 proteins connected with immunity and inflammation. One strand of the work used blood samples from two matched groups of 1,434 men each. Those in the first group were diagnosed with prostate cancer, typically more than ten years after giving blood, while those in the second stayed free of it. A second strand used records from 21,481 men in UK Biobank, 1,147 of whom had the disease, along with genetic techniques to test whether the links were likely to reflect cause and effect.

Interleukin-15, or IL-15, stood out. This protein helps to drive and sustain T cells and natural killer cells, both of which attack cancerous cells. Men with higher IL-15 had a 14% lower chance of a diagnosis made more than seven years afterwards. Where tumour details were available, the same protein was tied to a 26% lower chance of advanced disease. FLT3LG, BCL2L11, PGF, CKAP4 and TNFRSF11A showed comparable patterns.

A separate signal involved PARP1, which repairs DNA. Men whose genes predisposed them to higher PARP1 levels faced a greater risk of prostate cancer, and an independent, multiethnic group bore this out. Drugs that block PARP1 are already prescribed for some prostate cancers, though never to prevent them.

The researchers stress that these associations do not explain how the proteins behave as a cancer develops. Next, through the Prostate Cancer Aetiology and Prevention programme, they plan to examine how the proteins interact and whether influencing them changes risk.

## Why it matters

Prostate cancer has long been seen as "cold", meaning it was thought to stir up little immune response, and scientists have mostly studied immunity once a tumour has already formed. Evidence that immune activity matters years earlier points towards stepping in while men are still well, perhaps by putting existing medicines to new use. It is early days, and the findings mark out directions for research rather than a proven way to cut risk. Even so, for a cancer with no established preventive measures, a fresh line of inquiry is a welcome boost.
