A gentle, drug-free device worn on the ear has been used safely at home by children and teenagers during migraine attacks, according to a small early study from researchers at Nanfang Hospital and collaborating institutes. The results are preliminary, but they offer a first look at a nondrug option for young patients.

The technique is called transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation, or taVNS. It delivers mild electrical pulses to a branch of the vagus nerve, at the left cymba conchae, a part of the outer ear rich in vagal nerve endings. The vagus nerve is one of the body's most extensive cranial nerves, linking the brain with the internal organs and helping to regulate involuntary functions such as heart rate and digestion.

The study followed 11 patients aged between 7 and 18. Over four weeks, they gave themselves 30-minute sessions at home, covering 72 migraine attacks in all.

Safety looked reassuring. Across the 72 sessions, the team logged only two mild, short-lived skin reactions at the stimulation site, and no serious adverse events.

Pain scores generally drifted downwards during attacks. In 36.11% of treated episodes, patients were pain-free after two hours without taking any medication. Set against earlier baseline data, the young patients also had shorter attacks on average and fewer migraine days each month.

The researchers are admirably frank about the limits. The trial was open-label and single-arm, meaning there was no control group, so they cannot say the improvements came from the stimulation alone. Childhood migraine can vary a great deal and sometimes eases naturally, and expectation or placebo effects may play a part.

An exploratory look at heart rate variability also found no statistically significant changes once the analysis was corrected for multiple testing. For now, that means it cannot serve as a dependable marker of how well the treatment is working.

The team's next step is an adequately powered randomised trial that includes a sham-treatment comparison group.

## Why it matters

Migraine can weigh heavily on young people, yet treatments that avoid medication and suit children remain scarce. This study does not show that the device works, but it suggests families can use it safely in everyday life. That is a necessary first step before the larger, properly controlled trials that could show whether it genuinely helps.
