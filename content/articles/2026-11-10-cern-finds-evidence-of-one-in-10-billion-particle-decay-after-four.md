Scientists on the LHCb experiment at CERN have found evidence of a particle decay so scarce that it is expected only about once in every 10 billion decays of beauty particles. The result was presented at a seminar at CERN. It is the first time a single experiment has gathered enough evidence for this event.

The decay involves a particle called Bd, made from a beauty quark and a down antiquark. The team saw signs of it turning into a muon and an antimuon, and muons are essentially heavier cousins of the electron. The measurement fits the predictions of the Standard Model, the leading theory of fundamental particles, and it helps narrow the field of possible new particles beyond it.

Physicists prize such decays because of quantum effects. Other particles can nudge how often a decay happens, even ones far too heavy to be created in the Large Hadron Collider, and the rarer the decay, the more sensitive it tends to be to them.

The chase has been a long one. In 2015, LHCb and CMS jointly confirmed a related decay of a different beauty particle, the Bs, and spotted an early hint of the Bd version. Later work using data up to 2018 could not back that hint up. The Bs decay happens about three times per billion beauty decays, while the Bd decay is predicted to be around 30 times rarer still.

A major upgrade tipped the balance. From 2024, LHCb has collected data at five times the rate it managed between 2010 and 2018, with a detector that is better at telling particles apart. Machine learning then helped separate the faint signal from a mountain of other collision activity, no mean feat. Marco Santimaria, who co-led the 19-strong analysis team, stressed the extreme precision needed to pick out the muons.

Data from 2024 and 2025 gave a significance of 3.0 standard deviations. Adding the earlier 2011 to 2018 data lifted it to 3.2, above the threshold of three for evidence. A confirmed observation needs five, so the work is not finished. The same analysis also delivered the most precise measurements yet of the Bs decay.

Further gains are in store. The High Luminosity LHC is due to start running in 2030, and another big LHCb upgrade is scheduled beyond that. Together they could raise the rate of data collection by a further factor of ten.

## Why it matters

Physicists know the Standard Model cannot be the whole story, yet finding exactly where it falls short is notoriously difficult. Measuring very rare decays precisely is a way of detecting the faint influence of particles that no accelerator can yet make directly. Reaching evidence after four decades is a welcome boost, and the planned upgrades give researchers a realistic route to firmer answers.
