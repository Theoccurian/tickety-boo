The US Food and Drug Administration has approved Sunharbo (asundexian), a 50 mg tablet, to help prevent another stroke in adults who have recently had a mild-to-moderate noncardioembolic ischaemic stroke or a high-risk transient ischaemic attack (TIA). It is taken alongside antiplatelet therapy, not instead of it.

Sunharbo is the first factor XIa inhibitor to be approved for use with antiplatelet therapy, which is rather good news for stroke care.

The decision rests on OCEANIC-STROKE, a phase 3 trial involving 12,327 adults. All had either a noncardioembolic ischaemic stroke (scoring 15 or less on the NIH Stroke Scale) or a high-risk TIA. They were randomly assigned to Sunharbo or a placebo within 72 hours of their symptoms starting, and everyone also received antiplatelet therapy.

Over a median treatment period of 14.2 months, people taking 50 mg of Sunharbo once daily had a 26% lower risk of ischaemic stroke than those given placebo. That is a cracking result for a study of this size.

There are trade-offs. The overall rate of major bleeding was similar in both groups, but it was higher with Sunharbo in patients older than 75 and during the first 90 days of treatment. Gastrointestinal bleeding was also more common with the drug than with placebo, and diarrhoea was the most frequently reported side effect.

The approval comes with clear limits. Certain medicines that strongly induce both P-gp and CYP3A4 can weaken the tablet's effect, so that pairing is not recommended. Nor is Sunharbo advised for people with moderate or severe liver impairment, and it is not approved for stroke prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation.

Dr Ashkan Shoamanesh of McMaster University, co-principal investigator of the trial, noted that the danger of a further event is greatest in the days and weeks after a first stroke and lingers over time. He sees the drug as a valuable new option for patients recovering from a stroke.

## Why it matters

Surviving a stroke is only the first hurdle, because the danger of a second, potentially more damaging event remains high, particularly early on. Antiplatelet drugs are already a mainstay of prevention, so a medicine that adds protection when taken with them gives doctors another option. The bleeding findings mean prescribers will need to weigh benefits and risks carefully for each patient, especially older people.
