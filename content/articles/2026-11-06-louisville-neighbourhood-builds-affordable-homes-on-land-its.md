Residents of Smoketown, a historically Black neighbourhood in Louisville, Kentucky, have completed the first seven homes built by their own community land trust. The next seven plots have already been picked.

It began in 2019, when Nachand Trabue, a fourth-generation resident, spotted investors' signs offering to buy houses all over the area. Longstanding families were being priced out of a district with a proud history, including the city's first Black library and first Black dentist, and the birthplace of Muhammad Ali. Trabue started removing the signs, asked the city's land bank which vacant lots it held, and got in touch with River City Housing, a local nonprofit developer.

Meanwhile, the Smoketown Originals, a group of older residents with deep roots in the area, toured churches, community centres and youth centres to spread the word. They pushed the city to carry out a housing needs assessment and asked the national nonprofit Grounded Solutions whether a land trust could work.

Explaining the concept was a big part of the early effort. "We really had to rally the troops," says Theresa Boyd, the trust's executive director, who stresses that residents needed to see that their voices counted.

In 2020 the community and its partners won the city's call for proposals, which brought in $2.1 million. The trust was formally established in 2023. Its first homes stand opposite the spot where Ali threw his first punches. Before each new development, the trust invites the neighbours to share their worries and views.

The trust pays for the building work, but the city handed over the land free of charge. The land bank has earmarked around 70 plots for the trust, and residents secured that commitment in writing, no mean feat. Boyd says the city has been supportive, though years of turning up at council meetings helped get it there.

## Why it matters

Community land trusts hold on to the land beneath the homes, which lowers prices, and they require owners to resell at an affordable figure, so the benefit carries on past the first buyer. More than 300 now operate across the US. Smoketown shows how a neighbourhood facing speculation can use patience, local organising and expert partners to keep its land, and its future, in residents' hands.
