Shanghai has a new opera house on the banks of the Huangpu River, and its most eye-catching feature is a spiral staircase on the outside that anyone can climb, ticket or no ticket.

The Grand Opera House opened last weekend with a performance of Puccini's Tosca. Norwegian architecture firm Snøhetta was awarded the commission ten years ago, so this has been a long time coming, even if the curtain rose rather late in the year.

The staircase is inspired by a Chinese fan and winds up to an observation platform with a clear view of the central business district. It sits alongside the riverside thoroughfares and draws visitors towards the heart of the building's crescent-shaped floorplan.

The thinking behind it is simple. Snøhetta's team felt that opera houses, however splendid, are full of thresholds that limit how far people can enjoy and engage with the building. They set out to remove as many as they could and to sit more comfortably within the city around it.

Inside, the 1.6 million-square-foot (146,786-square-metre) building has three auditoriums with 4,200 seats in total. Performance, rehearsal, production, education, exhibition and hospitality all share the one roof.

The largest hall, Harmony, is dressed in red and has five levels and three terraces, seating 2,000 people. Its acoustics were calibrated with design partner Nagata Acoustics.

The Soar Theater has 1,200 seats and a more intimate feel. It suits baroque and classical opera, operetta, regional productions and theatre, and is likely to host traditional Chinese operas known as Xiqu.

The 1,000-seat Open Stage is built for experimental work, with adaptable stage and seating layouts. Floor-to-ceiling windows behind the stage look out on the river and the city, filling the hall with natural light.

The programme is already filling up. Verdi's Aida is on this weekend, and in three weeks the American Ballet Company arrives with Swan Lake.

## Why it matters

Opera houses can sometimes feel like closed worlds, reserved for those with a ticket and a sense that they belong. A public staircase with a view of the skyline invites everyone in, even if they never see a performance. Mixing traditional Chinese opera, Western classics and experimental work under one roof also gives audiences more ways to discover live performance.
