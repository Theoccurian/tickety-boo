SpaceX has, for the first time, plucked the upper stage of Starship out of the air as it came home from a mission lasting 22-and-a-half hours. The vehicle returned to Starbase in Boca Chica, Texas, and hovered beside its launch tower, where the tower's mechanical arms, nicknamed chopsticks, closed around it. Staff watching the flight let out loud cheers.

The 124m-tall (407ft) rocket lifted off at 16:47 local time on Friday. It was the 15th Starship test, and the first to aim at bringing back both of its main sections.

The booster, known as Super Heavy, did not join the celebration. It was meant to be caught about six-and-a-half minutes after launch, but an automated abort sent it towards the ocean instead. It hovered over the water and then exploded. Elon Musk later said on social media that fuel had been running a touch short.

The upper stage, called the Ship, pressed on into space and released 26 satellites that belong to the Starlink constellation, which supplies high-speed internet. It then orbited the Earth 14 times. SpaceX had said it would only try the catch once it had carefully checked the vehicle in space, and any fault would have meant a descent into the ocean, as happened with the booster.

Getting this far is no mean feat, as the Ship only reached orbit for the first time in September. The company had already caught the booster on three earlier test flights.

Prof Malcolm Macdonald, president of the Royal Aeronautical Society, described the Ship's return as "a substantial step forward". He pointed out that SpaceX has now proved it can recover each half of the vehicle, though not yet during a single flight. The next job, he said, is to combine the two and show that it can be done reliably, repeatedly and affordably.

## Why it matters

Rockets that can be flown again and again are the surest route to cheaper spaceflight. SpaceX has already seen this with its Falcon 9, whose reusable booster has sharply cut launch costs and now flies more than 100 times a year. Catching the Ship is a welcome boost for a much larger vehicle that Nasa has asked the company to adapt into a lander for astronauts on the Moon. The timetable for that work is still highly ambitious, but the catch shows real progress.
