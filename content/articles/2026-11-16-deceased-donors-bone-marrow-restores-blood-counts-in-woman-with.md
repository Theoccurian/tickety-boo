A woman of 40 with severe aplastic anaemia has had her blood counts restored after receiving bone marrow taken from a deceased organ donor. Doctors at Franciscan Health, a Catholic health care system in the American Midwest, say it is the first reported transplant of this kind for a non-malignant disease.

The patient had lived with the condition for ten years. It had been stable for a time, but it worsened to a severe form even after several courses of treatment. Once medication stopped helping, her care team decided on a transplant, so that healthy stem cells could take over the job of her failing marrow. The operation took place on 18 April.

Six months later, the news is rather good. The donated marrow has settled in and her blood counts have recovered, so she no longer needs transfusions of blood or platelets. Tests show full donor chimerism, which means every blood and immune cell she makes now comes from the donor's stem cells. There have been no complications.

The treating physician, Felix Mensah, noted that marrow transplants for severe aplastic anaemia have been carried out since the 1970s, using donors who are alive, whether relatives or unrelated. The novelty is the source. As far as he knows, nobody has previously reported using marrow from a deceased organ donor for this condition. He said the case underlines the need for more donor options, so that anyone with a life-threatening marrow failure disorder can reach a treatment that might cure them.

Kevin Caldwell, co-founder and chief executive of Ossium, which maintains a marrow bank, described the first non-malignant transplant of this type as a defining moment. He said that recovering and banking marrow from organ donors should help teams achieve reliably good outcomes.

Ossium intends to support more transplants this year, for both malignant and non-malignant diseases, through its HOPE Program. The aim is to widen access to deceased donor marrow for conditions where it brings clear clinical advantages.

## Why it matters

For marrow failure disorders such as severe aplastic anaemia, sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia, doctors generally favour stem cells drawn from marrow rather than from circulating blood. Finding a suitable living donor is not always possible, so a new source of marrow could widen the pool of options for patients. This is a single case, a welcome boost rather than a final verdict, and more patients and longer follow-up will show how widely the approach can be used.
