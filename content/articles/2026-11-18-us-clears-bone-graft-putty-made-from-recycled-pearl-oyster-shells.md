The US Food and Drug Administration has cleared a bone graft putty made from pearl oyster shells, a first for the field. PearlBone Putty 1600 has received what is known as 510(k) clearance, which means it can now be marketed and sold in America for repairing bone fractures.

No other replacement graft has been made from nacre, the mother-of-pearl layer lining an oyster shell. Nacre is mainly calcium carbonate, whereas human bone is built on calcium phosphate. The team behind PearlBone therefore patented a chemical reaction that swaps one for the other in around a day.

Minghao Zheng, a regenerative biologist at the University of Western Australia, is one of the researchers involved. He describes the method as a shortcut through a mineral-forming process that nature would take millions of years to complete.

The raw material is shell from Pinctada maxima oysters, sourced from Western Australian waters. It is ground into a powder, then mixed with a calcium phosphate salt and water. Better still, the shells are left over from the pearl farming industry and would otherwise be thrown away, which makes this a rather tidy bit of recycling.

The putty is meant to fill gaps while a bone mends, sometimes alongside plates or screws. Under the FDA's requirements, it cannot be relied upon to keep a bone stable by itself. The idea is that the body's own bone cells move into the space, following the structure of the material, which is in time replaced by ordinary bone. In the team's published laboratory work, the material held its strength and encouraged cells to attach and multiply.

Patients will have to wait a little longer. The company behind the product, Marine Biomedical, still needs to find partners in the US to manufacture it and sell it to clinics and commercial customers. Its chief executive, Rolee Kumar, said the clearance "reflects years of scientific research and extraordinary dedication".

## Why it matters

Bone grafts are a routine part of orthopaedic surgery, yet every current option involves some compromise. Taking bone from another part of the patient's body gives a good match but can mean pain and a longer operation, while other artificial substitutes may not take as well and can be limited in strength and in how quickly the body absorbs them. A material that is gradually replaced by real bone, and is made from a waste product, could ease some of those trade-offs. Clearance in the biggest orthopaedic market in the world is a cracking step along the way, even if hospitals are not quite there yet.
