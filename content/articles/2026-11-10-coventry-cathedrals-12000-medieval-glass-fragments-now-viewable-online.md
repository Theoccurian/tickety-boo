About 12,000 fragments of medieval stained glass from Coventry Cathedral have been photographed, catalogued and gathered into a digital exhibition. It means the public can again see a collection that has been out of general view for decades.

The glass was removed before the German air raids of the Second World War. When bombers destroyed the cathedral on 14 November 1940, it became the only British cathedral lost in the Blitz. The windows had been boxed up, 30 crates in all, and kept in a village rectory's cellars near the city until 1957.

The red sandstone cathedral was eventually rebuilt, while the burnt-out shell of the old building was left standing as a memorial to peace and reconciliation. The medieval windows, though, were never put back together. The World Monuments Fund and the cathedral's curators have now launched the exhibition on the Bloomberg Connects app, timed with the 86th anniversary of the raid.

This is Britain's largest assortment of loose medieval stained glass, and most of it dates from the 15th century. The pieces are attributed to John Thornton, a master glazier who also created the Great East Window at York Minster. They show episodes from the Passion of Christ, Apostles, saints, fanciful beasts and heraldic shields. Among the most distinctive are portraits of donors, ordinary men and women who paid for the windows, shown kneeling in prayer.

The glass had a bumpy history even before the war. It was partly smashed during the Reformation in the 16th century, and surviving scraps were later assembled into patchwork arrangements. Andrew Hines, the cathedral's head of heritage and interpretation, describes the wartime legacy as "a medieval jigsaw puzzle".

A first conservation effort recorded some of the glass in 2012, but the pieces then remained spread across three storage rooms for more than ten years. In the latest phase, stained-glass conservator Laura Atkinson led specialists in moving the collection into bespoke cabinets, logging tray numbers and taking high-resolution images for a digital database. Rather good news for historians, the exhibition is an early step in the cathedral's wider Building Hope project, which aims to preserve the ruins for future generations.

## Why it matters

Atkinson says the collection is of international importance yet has had little scholarly study. A full catalogue and public images are a first step towards opening it up to visitors and to researchers who can give it the attention it deserves. Alongside the ruins, the glass also adds to Coventry's story of survival, which the project's backers say still has much to be uncovered.
