An artificial intelligence tool that drafts heart ultrasound reports has proved as accurate as drafts written by people, and it left cardiologists with markedly less to do. The early findings from a randomised trial were presented in Chicago at a major American Heart Association meeting.

An echocardiogram is the most widely used form of heart imaging. Usually a sonographer captures the pictures, then a cardiologist studies them and writes a report on how the heart's structures and function look. Here, researchers tested whether AI could produce the first draft instead. They describe it as the first randomised trial of an AI tool writing a complete echocardiogram report, interpretations and measurements included.

The team gathered 1,200 scans taken during routine care at 21 Kaiser Permanente Northern California medical centres. For every scan, the AI tool, a sonographer and a cardiologist each wrote a preliminary report. The drafts were then handed out at random to cardiologists, who finalised them without being told who or what had produced the first version.

Accuracy was measured by how often cardiologists had to make substantial changes. They altered 50.6% of the AI drafts, against 49.7% of those from sonographers and cardiologists, which met the study's test for being comparable. The AI-assisted reports also fitted more closely with the historical reports in patients' records, and the reviewing doctors could not tell which drafts came from the AI.

The time saved was a cracking result. Finalising an AI-drafted report took cardiologists 4.6 minutes on average, compared with 8.5 minutes to produce a report without AI help, a saving of about 46%. Dr Keane K. Lee, a co-senior author and cardiologist at Kaiser Permanente Santa Clara Homestead Medical Center, said the scale of that saving came as a surprise. He suggested it could mean quicker results for patients and more time for doctors to spend with them.

He was also clear about where the technology stops. "AI cannot, however, replace the human team," he said, adding that the cardiologist stays responsible for the final report.

The researchers point to some caveats. The trial did not examine whether AI assistance improves patients' long-term health, and the cardiologists knew they were being studied, so they may not have worked quite as they normally would. These findings are preliminary, and further work is needed to see how the approach performs in everyday practice, with far more scans.

## Why it matters

Heart ultrasounds are among the most common cardiac tests, so a few minutes saved on each one adds up over a busy day. The source also cites projections of a US shortfall of about 7,000 cardiologists by 2036, which makes anything that frees up specialists' time particularly welcome. If the results hold at scale, AI drafting could help patients get answers sooner while doctors keep the final say.
