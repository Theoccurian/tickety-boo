Stone points unearthed beside a long-vanished lake in Saudi Arabia's Empty Quarter may once have been coated with plant poison to bring down large game. The site dates to about 125,000 years ago, and if the points are as old, they would predate any other known poisoned hunting gear by a wide margin. The research is published in the journal Science.

Today the site, Uruq Aljelibah, is dune country. Back then it sat beside a freshwater lake covering roughly 131 square miles and reaching nearly 100 feet deep, ringed by palms, reeds, trees and shrubs. Hippos, buffalo, ostriches, camels, gazelles and oryx all lived in the neighbourhood.

Archaeologists recovered more than 500 fossilised animal remains and over 5,500 stone artefacts. The points lay close to the bones, which suggests the shore was a place for hunting and butchering animals. Some were chipped in ways consistent with striking big beasts, and a few retained tiny fragments of animal tissue. Several also bore chemical signatures linked to the arrow-poison tree, whose compounds can alter heart rate.

Resin and fibres at the bases of the points indicate they were hafted, meaning they were mounted in composite weapons built from several materials. Huw Groucutt, a co-author from the University of Malta, said the team found direct residue evidence of how tools were fixed and used, which is seldom achieved at open-air sites in dry regions. That is no mean feat.

The team also spotted signs of cutting, scraping and fibre working, suggesting plants were gathered for food, tools and bindings. Jeffrey Rose of the University of Algarve, who was not involved, pointed out that some flakes can be fitted back together. That shows the tools were made right there on the lakeshore.

There are caveats. The points were lying on the surface rather than in datable layers of sediment, so they have not been dated directly. Nobody yet knows whether the poison worked or how it was prepared. No human remains were found, although the researchers suspect Homo sapiens lived at the site. The oldest confirmed poisoned weapons so far are around 60,000 years old.

## Why it matters

The Arabian Peninsula sits between Africa and the rest of Asia and probably served as a route for modern humans, especially in wetter spells. Lead author Chris Clarkson of Griffith University says the findings suggest people were not "just passing through", but settling green areas and using their resources with some skill. It is a welcome reminder that the thin surviving record may understate how clever our distant ancestors were.
