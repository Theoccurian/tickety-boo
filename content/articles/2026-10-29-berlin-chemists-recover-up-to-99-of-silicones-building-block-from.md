Chemists at the Institute of Chemistry at TU Berlin have found a way to split used silicone back into dimethyldichlorosilane, the molecule from which new silicone compounds are made. In tests on some everyday products, the team recovered as much as 99% of it.

Silicone is prized for its stability against heat and chemicals, and that is precisely why it resists recycling. Dr Martin Oestreich, who led the work, says most waste silicone has so far been burned or buried in landfill. Other recycling routes tend to produce ring-shaped siloxanes, which can go into new silicones, though these are generally of lower quality than the originals.

The Berlin approach goes further by breaking the long chains of alternating silicon and oxygen atoms apart completely. Oestreich says this is the hard part. "Breaking the silicon-oxygen bonds is extremely difficult."

The method uses a silylium ion, a compound with a positively charged silicon atom, as a catalyst. It works alongside phosgene, a toxic gas, to create a molecule able to snap those bonds. To keep matters manageable, the researchers used solid triphosgene, which releases phosgene only during the reaction. The oxygen freed from the chains escapes at once as carbon dioxide.

The team tried the process on silicone cupcake pans, baby bottle teats, lids and other household items. At 80 degrees, and without significant energy input, some products gave a 99% yield after 24 hours. A mixture of different used silicone products yielded 86%, which is a cracking result for such a jumble.

A patent application has been filed, and the journal Nature chose the paper for fast-track online publication. The researchers are already working on alternatives to phosgene. They also plan to develop ways of removing even the smallest traces of it from the recovered dimethyldichlorosilane.

Oestreich's group has studied silylium ions for 15 years, and only in the last five or so did silicone recycling emerge as a key application.

## Why it matters

Silicone turns up in everything from baking pans to medical tubing, and making it takes a great deal of energy: the silicon must first be extracted from quartz sand at 2,000 degrees Celsius. The source also points to growing evidence that silicone in landfill can harm the environment and human health. Getting back to the original building block, rather than a lower-grade material, means used silicone could be turned into new silicones with a range of properties.
