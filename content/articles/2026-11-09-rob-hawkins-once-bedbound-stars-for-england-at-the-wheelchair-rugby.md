Rob Hawkins spent 18 months confined to his bed with chronic fatigue syndrome. Now the 25-year-old is among the finest wheelchair rugby league players in the world, and he is on the wing for England in a World Cup semi-final against hosts Australia in Wollongong.

He found the sport entirely by accident in 2014. Having spotted wheelchair basketball on television, he and his family hunted for a local club and went along to what they took to be a Halifax basketball session. It turned out to be wheelchair rugby league, and he decided to stay.

Before he became ill, Hawkins had been a keen runner and footballer. The illness left him relying on walking frames, or on relatives just to get to the toilet, and he missed two years of high school. His first session in a sports chair lasted around 20 minutes before his body called time, yet he was eager to return. Learning that he could play for his country, he says, revived a competitive streak he had not felt since falling ill.

The honours have followed. Hawkins plays for Halifax Panthers and England, and he was voted International Wheelchair Player of the Year in 2024 and 2025, collecting the IRL Golden Boot each time. He is also the reigning Wheels of Steel winner, the award for the best player in Super League. He was a big presence in the England side that won the World Cup in Manchester four years ago.

Life with the condition still calls for care. Some days he rests from morning until night, and over the years he has learned to pace himself. He goes as far as saying he is glad of his illness, because it has brought him into contact with people he might never otherwise have met.

England go into the semi-final as favourites, a splendid position for the defending champions. They topped Pool A with victories of 98-18 over Wales, 134-0 against the USA and 68-32 against Ireland. Australia, who ran England close in the 2025 Ashes, lost 69-34 to France in the group stage.

In the England team room, the squad has been spray-painting a ladder gold after every win. "I'm excited for the pressure," said Hawkins. A victory would move England a step closer to becoming the first nation to win the trophy three times.

## Why it matters

Hawkins' story shows that a long-term illness need not end sporting ambition, and that adaptive sport can offer people purpose, competition and a sense of belonging. His candour about rest and pacing is a useful reminder that elite performance and living with a chronic condition can sit side by side.
