Scientists at Cortical Labs in Australia have given a robotic dog a brain of sorts: a small cluster of lab-grown neurons that steers it towards a target on a simple map. It is a rather neat, if early, demonstration of computing with living cells.

The neurons were grown directly on a chip studded with electrodes. These carry electrical signals in both directions, so the team could turn the activity of the cells into movement in the robot.

The attraction is easy to grasp. Artificial intelligence is hugely capable, yet the human brain manages on far less power and copes much better with unfamiliar situations. Chips built from living neurons could eventually offer savings in energy and greater flexibility.

Cortical Labs has form in this area. Its neuron clusters have already completed computer tasks, including playing Pong and Doom, and the dog takes the idea into the physical world. Brett Kagan, the co-founder and CEO, says modern robots are remarkably good at moving about but still find it hard to learn quickly or adjust when circumstances change.

A companion experiment with Honda Research Institute Europe took place entirely in software. A virtual character had to find its way across a simple grid to a reward, with electrical pulses sent to the chip and replies coming back. By altering the pulses and giving feedback on each move, the researchers trained the living chip and read its responses.

Over more than 4,000 hours of interaction, they tried almost 1,300 different signal combinations.

Plenty remains to be done. The technology is not especially quick or efficient yet, and the work involved a fair amount of trial and error. Next, the team wants to scale up the chips so they can handle harder tasks in less time, and to find out how they hold on to information, including what kind of memory they have.

The navigation study has not yet been peer-reviewed. It was shown at the NeurIPS 2026 conference and posted on the arXiv website.

## Why it matters

Robots that work in remote or hazardous places need to make sensible decisions and keep learning as conditions change. The human brain does this on a modest energy budget, so computers that borrow from living neurons could one day be both greener and more adaptable. For now this is a simple proof of concept, but it shows that biological chips can be linked to real machines and trained to direct them.
