Solar panels supplied 28% of Pakistan's electricity in 2025, up from just 1% in 2018, according to the Pakistan-based think tank Renewables First. Some seven million households now own panels, and they glisten from the roofs of homes and hospitals, from farmers' fields and from industrial complexes.

The boom was driven by ordinary families rather than by government policy. Most people interviewed said they had installed panels to escape soaring bills from an often unreliable national grid. Muhammad Hassan Dajana, industry liaison at Renewables First, says tariffs have more than doubled in a few years, while the cost of Chinese solar panels has fallen by 93%.

In rural Punjab, newly married Faisal Shahzad keeps a 340W panel under the bed, part of the dowry from his bride's family. Over the summer, he says, the household ran its air cooler from it all day without using grid power. Panels and coolers have replaced motorbikes as popular wedding gifts. "It has almost become a must," he says.

Industry is following suit. At Kohinoor Textiles Mills in Islamabad, the machines run entirely on solar power whenever the sun is shining, and the old furnace-oil engines sit mothballed in a warehouse. Industrial batteries are due within weeks to store surplus energy, though the firm does not plan to cut its grid connection altogether.

Going from 1% to 28% in seven years is no mean feat, and it brings real challenges. As more people leave the grid, fewer customers are left to pay for its upkeep, which can push prices up and tempt still more people towards solar. Energy Minister Awais Ahmad Leghari says that over the past two years this exodus has cost the remaining consumers more than $500 million a year.

The government says it does not want to pull people away from solar. Instead, it wants a more competitive grid, with better storage, less waste and more innovation, and it says it has renegotiated most contracts with private power producers. It has also changed the rules on feeding surplus solar power back into the grid, a move critics say hurts small and medium businesses. Mr Leghari's goal is for up to 90% clean power, including renewables and nuclear, by the end of 2036.

## Why it matters

Pakistan shows how quickly cheap solar can spread when people have a strong financial reason to adopt it, trimming household bills and reducing reliance on imported fuel. The harder task now is keeping the grid affordable for those who cannot buy panels. Mr Dajana describes two paths ahead: solar and the grid working side by side as a model of gradual transition, or a costly, declining grid that becomes a cautionary tale.
