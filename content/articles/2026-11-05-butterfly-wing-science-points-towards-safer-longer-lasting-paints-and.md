Chemists at Northeastern University in Boston are turning to butterflies for help in making paints and glitters that do not depend on harmful, chemical-based pigments.

Their interest lies in structural colour. Rather than using a dye to soak up some light and return the rest, microscopic structures play with light directly, producing the shimmer seen on butterfly wings and peacock feathers.

Most of the colour around us does come from pigments. A carrot's orange, for instance, comes from carotenoids, which send back orange light and absorb the rest. The snag is that pigments can fade in strong sun, and some man-made ones contain lead, cobalt or arsenic compounds, which may endanger health and the natural world.

Butterfly colour works differently. It comes from minuscule crystals that bend and scatter light, so only certain wavelengths bounce back to our eyes. Leila Deravi, an associate professor of chemistry and chemical biology at the university, is examining pterin crystals taken from the wings of the cabbage white butterfly. She thinks the colour could be tuned by altering the size and arrangement of those crystals.

Pterins have not yet been formally put through safety assessments. Even so, because they occur naturally, Deravi's team considers them a promising subject for closer study. Deravi herself describes them as gentle on people and the environment, without the lingering toxicity of the forever chemicals found in some synthetic dyes.

Scaling up is the next hurdle, and no small one. So far the team has grown only tiny crystals, weighing a few milligrams and comparable to a single speck of table salt. Deravi nonetheless believes the method could one day be used commercially.

She is also frank that the science is far from settled. Researchers are still working out exactly how the long-lasting, fade-resistant colours of living things come about, even though we see them daily in flowers, insects and other animals.

## Why it matters

Paints and glitters are part of everyday life, yet many depend on pigments that fade or on ingredients that can harm people and the planet. Colour built from structure rather than chemistry could, in time, mean coatings that last longer and are kinder to health and the environment. It is early days, with safety checks and large-scale production still to come, but nature has handed researchers a splendid blueprint to work from.
