Adults in New York City with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure ate better, worried less about getting enough food and saw their blood sugar and blood pressure fall after an eight-month programme of medically tailored meals. The early findings were presented at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago, and they are rather good news for anyone weighing up the cost of such schemes.

Nour Makarem of Columbia University's Mailman School of Public Health led the study, which involved 110 adults with an average age of 63. Participants were recruited from neighbourhoods with high rates of chronic disease and premature death, and the work ran between 2024 and 2026.

Half of the group received the standard dose of 10 meals a week for the full eight months. The others had that same amount for three months, then one fewer meal each month, finishing on five a week in the last month. Everyone also saw a dietitian and took part in a heart health education programme, with cooking demonstrations, fresh produce and healthy seasonings to use at home.

Both groups improved. Diet quality climbed by about three points on a scale that gauges how closely people follow a Mediterranean-style way of eating. Participants were also more than twice as likely to reach a higher food security status as they had been at the start.

Blood sugar, measured by HbA1c testing, dropped by about 0.5%. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure each fell by roughly 4 mm Hg. The group whose meals were tapered off enjoyed similar gains to those on the full dose, which is no mean feat.

Kevin Volpp, who leads the American Heart Association's Health Care by Food initiative and was not involved in the research, pointed out that tailored meals are costly. A gradual taper, he suggested, could make programmes easier to sustain and allow them to reach more people.

The researchers are careful to add caveats. The trial took place at one location in New York City, so the results may not carry over everywhere, and larger studies across several sites are needed to confirm them.

## Why it matters

Poor diet is a leading risk factor for heart and metabolic disease, and the source notes that more than half of adults in the US have a diet of poor quality. Schemes that treat food as medicine have usually been aimed at people who are already seriously ill, whereas this study points towards stepping in earlier, before conditions worsen. If a tapered approach backed by good education can hold on to the benefits, it could make these programmes affordable enough to help many more people.
