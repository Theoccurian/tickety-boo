Future lunar bases could one day be built partly from the moon's own surface. Engineers at the University of Arkansas have 3D-printed small interlocking bricks from simulated moon dust, with no glue-like additive mixed in.

The team used a method known as laser powder bed fusion. A thin layer of fine powder is spread over a flat platform, and an intense beam of light heats the grains until they fuse together, a process called sintering. The platform then sinks slightly, a fresh layer of powder goes on top, and the cycle repeats.

The stand-in for moon dust was Lunar Highlands Regolith Simulant, which is sold commercially and designed to imitate the samples gathered during the Apollo missions. Genuine regolith is the loose, jagged blanket covering the moon, ground out over billions of years by asteroids, micrometeorites and other debris. Unlike soil on Earth, it contains no organic matter.

Earlier attempts to print with regolith have generally mixed in binding agents, and those would need to be carried up from Earth. According to the Arkansas team, theirs is the first demonstration of fusing the simulant directly into bricks that can be fitted together and taken apart again, using a light source of comparatively modest power. The work appears in the journal npj Advanced Manufacturing.

The printed pieces are a few centimetres wide and come in two shapes, one resembling a cross and the other the letter H. Like LEGO bricks, they can be slotted together to form larger structures.

There are limits, naturally. Bricks made from pure regolith are more porous and weaker than those made with binders. They can hold themselves up, but first author Cole McCallum says he would be wary of claiming they could bear other loads just yet.

Senior author Wan Shou, an assistant professor of mechanical engineering, says further study is needed to see how the process behaves in microgravity and in a vacuum. The strength of the bricks will also need to improve before they are of practical use. He remains optimistic that the approach could offer an efficient way to make parts for a permanent moon settlement.

McCallum was still an undergraduate when the research was done. He has since graduated in mechanical engineering and physics, and is now studying aerospace engineering in Toulouse as one of 30 inaugural Lafayette Fellows, a cracking start to a research career.

## Why it matters

Everything launched from Earth adds cost and complexity to a space mission, so making use of materials already on the moon is an attractive aim. Leaving out the binder takes one item off the shipping list. These bricks are an early step, made from a simulant under Earth conditions, but they hint at a more self-sufficient way of building away from our own planet.
