High in the Tunari mountains of central Bolivia, Indigenous Quechua communities have planted nearly 500,000 native Kewiña seedlings since 2022. The project is bringing back a forest that helps secure water, and it is teaching local women to read and write along the way.

The scheme is run by the Bolivian nonprofit Faunagua and involves six communities. Villagers raise young trees in shade houses before moving them to the surrounding hillsides. According to Faunagua's executive director, Rosmery Ayala, 110,000 seedlings were grown this year alone.

The trees are sorely needed. Biologists estimate that just 10% of the region's original Kewiña cover remains. Trees were felled for fuel and building timber, and to make room for potato fields, while city growth and wildfires added to the losses. An attempt in the 1960s to tackle erosion with non-native pines and eucalyptus only made water shortages worse.

Kewiña, a slow-growing relative of the rose, behaves quite differently. Its small, dense leaves catch droplets from mountain fog and guide them into the soil, and it loses very little moisture to the air. That matters because studies suggest around 80% of the Cochabamba metropolitan area's water supply traces back, one way or another, to catchment areas within Tunari National Park. Residents of the village of Chiaraje say they have already noticed more rain and humidity.

The project has a social side as well. Faunagua found that women living in scattered homes had few places to meet, so it helped create one. When some women said they had never had the chance to learn to read, literacy classes were added to the programme.

About 25 women from Chiaraje, a village of roughly 300 people, formed a group in 2023 called the Warmi Kewiñas, which means "Kewiña Women" in Quechua. Its president, potato farmer Andrea Vicente, says all of the members have now learned to read, write and sign their names. Men take part in the tree work too. A small building has also been finished that will serve as a daycare centre while the women tend saplings in the nearby shade house.

Nobody is claiming the job is done. The initiative is limited by its scale, and one sociologist points out that many community forestry schemes falter once funding dries up. Ayala argues that education and a real sense of ownership are the best protection, since Kewiña trees take decades to mature.

## Why it matters

Mountain forests that gather fog and shield groundwater can matter far beyond the slopes where they grow, including for a city downstream. By planting native species and investing in education and a stronger voice for women, this project aims to keep communities committed for the decades the trees need. Whether it succeeds remains to be seen, but it is a promising start, and no mean feat.
