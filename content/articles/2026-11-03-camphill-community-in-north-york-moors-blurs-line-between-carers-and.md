In Botton, a village in the North York Moors, people with and without learning disabilities share homes, meals and daily work. The Esk Valley Camphill Community there has around 120 residents living across 19 households, and it offers a rather different picture of what social care can look like.

The community is one of two organisations based in the village, the other being the Camphill Village Trust. It belongs to the Camphill movement, an international network of communities. Their guiding belief is that those with learning disabilities and those without can build a life together as neighbours, not as customers and suppliers of a service.

Botton was founded in 1955 by Karl König, an Austrian paediatrician who had begun the movement in rural Scotland 16 years earlier. At its peak the village was home to roughly 300 to 400 people.

Each house has two or three co-workers, a similar number of residents known as villagers, and usually one volunteer. The day follows a shared rhythm: breakfast at about 7.30am, a workshop from 9am until noon, lunch, a second workshop, then dinner at around 6.30pm. The workshops include gardening, woodwork, crafts, cooking and forestry.

One simple habit does a great deal of work. People move between houses for lunch and dinner, so roughly half of everyone's meals are eaten at other households' tables. That keeps the 19 homes from becoming 19 separate units and weaves a web of daily relationships through the village.

A philosophy PhD researcher from the University of Sheffield came to know the place after visiting a school friend, Max, who lived there. The researcher went on to volunteer for three months, living in one of the houses and, most weeks, spending four half-days in the garden workshop.

Early on, a co-worker asked a resident called Linda, officially someone receiving care, to show the newcomer how the village's transport arrangements worked. She did so with enthusiasm and knew every detail. The researcher came to see that people receiving support were often giving it too.

Max had previously tried a special needs school and a service-based care arrangement that struggled to accommodate him. In Botton he moved about with a settled ease his friend had not seen before, and leaving the house unsupervised, once difficult, was no longer the same obstacle. "It's been the best two years of my life," he said.

## Why it matters

The UK's social care system faces well-documented pressures, and the researcher began to wonder whether a deeper question lay beneath them: must care always be a service supplied by people employed to provide it? Botton shows one alternative, in which support grows out of shared daily life and people both give and receive. It is a single community's experience rather than a blueprint, but it invites a useful rethink of how care might be organised.
