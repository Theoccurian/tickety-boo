A smartwatch paired with an artificial intelligence system can pick up signs of high blood pressure about as well as a standard check in a doctor's office, according to research published in Nature. The team, which includes Ming-Zher Poh, tested the approach on a Google smartwatch of the kind anyone can buy.

The watch uses an optical sensor to follow changes in blood volume in the tiny vessels beneath the skin, while an accelerometer records how the wrist moves. Those signals are combined with the wearer's height and passed to an algorithm, which estimates the probability of hypertension. Notably, no calibration against a traditional cuff is needed, although the estimate does depend on several readings.

The system learned from nearly 500,000 hours of smartwatch data gathered from 2,109 people, with further training and testing in several other groups. It was then put through its paces in 196 participants. For that final test, both the watch's estimates and ordinary office readings were measured against a full day of ambulatory monitoring, which is regarded as the most dependable clinical benchmark.

The two approaches came out closely matched. The watch correctly flagged 65.8% of hypertension cases, compared with 55.3% for office cuff monitoring, though the gap was not statistically significant. Both were equally good at avoiding false alarms, doing so 90% of the time.

The authors are clear that the watch would not take the place of cuff-based monitoring. Instead, they see it as a tool for identifying people who show signs of hypertension, which could lead to earlier diagnosis and treatment and may help cut the number of cardiovascular events.

## Why it matters

High blood pressure is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, yet more than half of the adults affected are unaware they have it. A device that many people already wear on the wrist could be a handy way of nudging them towards a proper check. This is a single study, and the watch is a screening aid rather than a diagnostic tool, but matching routine office screening is rather good news for wearable health technology.
