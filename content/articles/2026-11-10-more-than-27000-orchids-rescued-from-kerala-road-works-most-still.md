A rescue mission for tree-dwelling orchids in the southern Indian state of Kerala has worked, according to a recent study. Researchers gathered 27,324 plants from trees that had been felled, or were about to be, for road development. When they checked again a year or two later, nearly 71% were still alive.

Many orchids are epiphytes, which means they live on trees and rely on them for support. Lose the tree and the orchid frequently goes with it. The team relocated the rescued plants to three public places: Kappad Beach, the urban mangrove wetland at Sarovaram Bio Park, and Malabar Botanical Garden, another city wetland.

Checks made 12 to 24 months later found more than 19,500 survivors. The rescue included 12 species found only in the region, and 6,126 of the plants moved were of these endemic kinds. Nearly 55% of them pulled through. Eleven of those 12 species have never been assessed for the IUCN Red List, so how threatened they are remains unknown.

The move was a gamble. Viswambharan Sarasan, a co-author from the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, explained that many of the endemic species naturally grow in cool, hilly country, while the new homes were warmer and close to sea level. Even so, many of the transplants bloomed and went on to form seed capsules, which took him by surprise. That is a cracking result for a rescue carried out in a hurry.

The team also used the project to get people interested. At Sarovaram, ten of the species were tagged with QR codes that visitors could scan to find out about the plants and how they came to be there. The park welcomes nearly 511,000 fee-paying visitors a year, and the tagged orchids were scanned more than 126,000 times over two years. Sarasan was struck that modest orchids with tiny flowers drew just as much attention as the showy ones.

The authors conclude that relocating orchids can turn plants facing certain destruction into valuable conservation assets. They stress, however, that it should be a last resort, used only when the trees carrying them cannot be kept.

## Why it matters

Kerala is home to 271 known orchid species, many of them growing on trees across the biodiverse Western Ghats. Sarasan believes some orchids are vanishing unnoticed, and nobody knows how many have been lost to road building over the past 15 to 20 years. This study offers a practical way to save at least some of them when development cannot be avoided, and it shows that public curiosity can become part of the conservation effort.
