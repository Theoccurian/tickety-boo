A plain plastic filter has managed to sort crude oil at room temperature, and it had some unexpected help from the oil itself. Researchers at KAIST in Korea and Georgia Tech have demonstrated the method, and their results appear in Nature.

The project was led by Professor Dong-Yeun Koh of KAIST, working with Professor Ryan Lively's research group at Georgia Tech.

Refineries have long relied on distillation, which heats crude oil above 350 °C so that parts of it become vapour and can be gathered as they cool. Globally, the process swallows around 1,100 terawatt-hours a year. That is on a par with what about 130 one-gigawatt nuclear power plants generate in twelve months.

Membranes have been a tempting alternative, but they have usually needed an ultra-thin specialist coating. Such coatings are costly and can develop flaws when made in bulk. This team left the coating out altogether and used bare porous polyacrylonitrile, an inexpensive, chemically stable polymer already familiar in industrial filtration.

As crude oil moved through, heavier hydrocarbon molecules settled on the inner walls of the pores. That narrowed the gaps into channels less than 2 nanometres across, which let lighter fractions such as naphtha, gasoline and kerosene pass quickly while slowing heavier material.

Clogging of this sort, known as fouling, is normally a headache that ruins filters. Here it did the sorting. The membrane's flow rate was roughly 23 times higher than that of earlier leading designs, and it worked steadily for 28 days in a row. Professor Lively said industry "should seriously consider adopting the technology".

Computer simulations suggest the membrane could be fitted as a modular stage ahead of existing distillation kit, so refineries would not have to start afresh. The modelling projects energy use down by 31.6%, carbon dioxide emissions down by 37.6%, cooling water use down by 20.7% and operating costs down by 36%. The researchers estimate that using it across Korea's refining and petrochemical industries could avoid about 10 million tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions a year.

Those numbers come from simulations, not working refineries. Next, the team plans to improve large-scale membrane production and prove it stays reliable over longer runs. They also see possible uses in recycling plastic waste, recovering solvents in battery manufacturing, purifying pharmaceuticals and making biofuels.

## Why it matters

Oil refining is among the most energy-intensive industries in the world, and it supplies the raw materials for plastics and countless everyday goods as well as fuel. A cheaper, lower-energy way to handle part of the job could trim both bills and emissions without refineries being rebuilt. Scaling up will be no mean feat, but this is rather promising news.
