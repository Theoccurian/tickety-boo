Endangered bogong moths can steer by the Earth's magnetic field, according to a new study in Nature. The work, led by Eric Warrant of Lund University in Sweden, helps explain how these insects keep to their course on nights when cloud hides the stars.

Each year, masses of the moths emerge from underground cocoons in Australia's south-east outback and fly up to 1,000 kilometres south. Every moth makes the trip just once in its short life, yet they all find their way to a small number of caves in the Australian Alps. They spend the summer there in the shade, in a dormant state, until the cooler turn of autumn calls them back to breed.

Warrant's team showed last year that the moths can navigate by starlight alone. Stars, though, are frequently blocked by cloud, while the magnetic field is far less often missing, even if it does sometimes shift, flip and waver. The moths seem undeterred by overcast skies, which hinted that they had a second compass.

To test the idea, the researchers caught moths in light traps on Mount Selwyn, about 70 kilometres north of the caves, during spring and autumn migrations. The insects were then tethered inside a flight simulator in a dark laboratory built without magnetic metal. Around each moth stood cardboard silhouettes of trees, grass and hills, while a moving pattern on the floor mimicked the ground passing beneath it and a projector painted the sky.

The whole arena sat inside a coil that produces a controlled magnetic field. When the stars were deliberately scrambled, moths still flew in the right direction so long as the natural field was in place. Cancel the field and they became entirely disoriented. Turn it through 180 degrees, or invert its inclination, and they flew the opposite way.

Weak radiofrequency noise also left the moths confused, as did filtering out violet-blue light. Both are known to disrupt navigation in other magnetic navigators, such as European robins and monarch butterflies. The team suspects the moths' sense relies on a light-triggered chemical reaction, although the organ responsible has yet to be found.

## Why it matters

Finding out how a young insect, with no previous experience of the route, crosses a huge stretch of country is no mean feat, and this study adds a rather important piece to the puzzle. It suggests the moths carry more than one compass, so a cloudy night or a magnetic disturbance need not leave them lost. The researchers say how these different cues are combined in the insects' brains is a question for future investigation.
