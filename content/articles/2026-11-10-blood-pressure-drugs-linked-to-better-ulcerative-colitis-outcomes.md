A familiar class of blood pressure tablets may help a leading ulcerative colitis treatment work better, according to research led by Northwestern Medicine in the US and Kiel University in Germany. Among patients given vedolizumab (sold as Entyvio), those who also used an ACE inhibitor or an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) for hypertension had a lower chance of being admitted to hospital or having their colon removed.

Ulcerative colitis is the most common form of inflammatory bowel disease. It is a long-term condition that inflames the colon and causes sores, and it affects over 1 million people in the US. It often begins in the teenage years or early adulthood, and even with advanced, costly therapies such as vedolizumab, most patients do not reach lasting remission.

The researchers first examined data on more than 1,300 patients from two clinical trials of vedolizumab. By following changes in stool frequency and rectal bleeding, they could sort people into distinct patterns of response. Tissue biopsies and blood samples were then checked for gene and protein activity to find out why some patients did poorly.

Two discoveries followed. A weak response to the drug was tied to a form of inflammation involving the blood vessels of the colon. That inflammation, in turn, was connected to a biological pathway already targeted by ACE inhibitors and ARBs.

To test the idea, the team turned to health care records for nearly 9,000 people who had both ulcerative colitis and high blood pressure. Among those using vedolizumab, patients who also had one of these two drug types went into hospital less often and needed fewer operations.

The effect looked quite specific. Other classes of blood pressure medicine showed no such pattern, and people who took ACE inhibitors or ARBs alongside different colitis treatments saw no difference.

The findings, published in Nature Medicine, come from analysing existing data, so clinical trials are needed to confirm them. Those trials must also address patients with normal blood pressure, who would not want a medicine that lowers it. Senior author Dr Parambir Dulai says the work gives a reason to consider developing drugs aimed squarely at the same pathway, without the blood pressure effects.

His team is also building a diagnostic test around the biological markers found in patients who did not respond to vedolizumab. A test of that sort could let doctors work out in advance who is likely to benefit and who might be better served by an alternative.

## Why it matters

Ulcerative colitis can disrupt school, work and social life for young people, and finding a treatment that helps can mean months of continuing symptoms. Repurposing inexpensive generic medicines, or designing new ones for the same pathway, could widen access to effective care. A test that predicts who will respond would also spare patients time on treatments unlikely to help. It is early days, but it is a welcome lead.
