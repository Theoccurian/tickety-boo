A man in Oxford, Ohio, who had put up with years of hate mail about his Halloween decorations has been thanked by a woman for brightening her journeys to cancer treatment. He answered her kindness with a surprise of his own.

Tammy Wyche drove past Bill Pyle's house every day on her way to radiation appointments. His front yard was packed with skeletons arranged in elaborate scenes. One group raised the flag at Iwo Jima, another skeleton handed a bone to its dog, and a beekeeper stood behind a cowboy riding a skeletal horse. Close by, two tourists posed for a selfie.

Wyche marvelled at the creativity on show. Pyle, however, had grown used to a far less appreciative audience. In the weeks before each Halloween, his mailbox brought accusations of devil worship and predictions that he would soon be heading for hell. It went on year after year.

Then a handwritten letter from Wyche arrived. She explained that she had passed his house on 19 days running, and that spotting which new skeletons had joined the display was a highlight of her trip. Her final treatment fell on Wednesday 11 September, she wrote, although she might still drive by now and then to see what had changed.

Pyle said the letter stopped him short. "Tears came to my eyes," he said.

Noticing the date of her last session, he set about planning something special. When Wyche drove past that Wednesday morning, she glanced to her right and found a whole crew of skeletons holding signs that celebrated the end of her treatment. She said she was moved that anyone would go to such lengths for a stranger.

The story has since travelled well beyond Ohio. For every person who now complains about the display, there are 30 sending messages of appreciation, some from as far away as Australia. Those messages urge Pyle to keep going with his skeletons, which is rather good news for a man whose mailbox had been the scariest part of the season.

## Why it matters

Cancer treatment can be long and gruelling, and small moments of delight along the way can make a daily routine easier to bear. This story shows how a simple, generous gesture towards someone you have never met can lift a difficult stretch, and how a single kind word can outweigh years of unkind ones.
