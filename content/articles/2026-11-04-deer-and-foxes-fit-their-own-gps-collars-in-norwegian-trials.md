Researchers in Norway have found a way for wild deer and foxes to wear tracking collars without ever being caught. The technique, called Capture-Free Telemetry, was devised at the Norwegian University of Life Sciences (NMBU). Animals fit the GPS devices to themselves while investigating baited units placed in their natural surroundings.

For some sixty years, following an animal by satellite has meant trapping it first. That is invasive, costly and hard work, and being restrained can badly stress the animal. Injury or death is possible, and people are sometimes put at risk too.

Professor Richard Bischof, who invented the system and wrote the study, built proof-of-concept versions in Norway: one for red deer and one for red foxes. The deer trials were held at the Norwegian Deer Centre. The findings are published in BioScience.

Welfare is built into the design. Each collar adjusts on its own to suit the animal's neck. It is meant to detach by itself once the tracking period is over, which runs from several weeks to a couple of months, and it has deliberately fragile points so that it can part company if the animal is uncomfortable or gets tangled.

Bischof says that catching and immobilising animals alters their behaviour, physiology and fitness, which rather undermines research into natural behaviour. Since capture is the trickiest step, studies often follow only a handful of animals. He envisages "finer-touch telemetry": more animals, collared less invasively, each followed for a shorter time.

The idea has deep roots. Before moving to Norway for his doctorate, Bischof worked as a wildlife biologist in Nebraska, alongside professional trappers, and began to wonder whether snare-based devices might mark animals without anyone handling them. He later unearthed largely forgotten studies from the 1950s to the 1970s on automatic marking, which gave the modern version a sturdy foundation.

Next, Bischof and colleagues in Europe and the United States plan to compare the behavioural effects of capture-free collaring with those of the conventional approach. They also want to explore whether it could suit other species.

## Why it matters

Much of what we know about how wildlife moves, and therefore how best to protect it, comes from tracking collars. If animals can be fitted with them without being chased, restrained or handled, researchers could improve welfare and gather information that better reflects natural behaviour. It is early days, but collaring more animals with less disturbance would be a welcome step for conservation science.
