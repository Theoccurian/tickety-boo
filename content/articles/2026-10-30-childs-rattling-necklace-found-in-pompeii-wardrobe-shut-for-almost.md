A wooden wardrobe that stayed closed for almost 2,000 years has given up its contents at Pompeii. Among them are a small perfume bottle, makeup tools and a child's necklace strung with charms.

The wardrobe stood in a room of a house on Via dell'Abbondanza, one of the liveliest streets in the Roman city before Mount Vesuvius erupted in AD79. Archaeologists also lifted out tweezers, an incense burner and a bronze lantern.

The necklace is the real standout. It carries more than 20 tiny charms made from different materials and showing several deities, including Bes and Mercury. Known as a crepundia, this kind of necklace was usually worn by children. The charms rattled, so parents could keep track of a youngster, and they might also help to identify a lost child.

Gabriel Zuchtriegel, director of the Pompeii archaeological park, explained that such a piece could keep a child linked to parents who might be living far away. That suited a society in which plenty of people travelled around the Mediterranean and took keepsakes with them.

The wardrobe further held 38 bronze coins, cooking pots and a wooden box of lead weights, which hints that someone in the household earned a living as a tradesperson. Zuchtriegel said that leaving money and belongings behind could indicate the occupants had to escape the eruption in a hurry. In the storage area, the team also came across the broken skeletons of two mice, inside an amphora.

Inscriptions once connected the house with a man called Cornelius Maximus, although they can no longer be seen. The front of the building was dug in 1912, but specialists are only now turning to its interior rooms, as one strand of a broader restoration project.

Elsewhere, excavations are continuing at the Villa of the Mysteries, on the edge of the city. The team is deliberately avoiding the owners' living quarters, where grand frescoes might be expected, and is working in the servants' area instead. Zuchtriegel said the villa has never been fully excavated, and he hopes the work will shed light on the daily lives and economy of people who stood outside the spotlight of history. Most of the villa's inhabitants, he noted, were servants.

## Why it matters

Grand buildings and fine frescoes tend to shape our picture of the ancient world, but everyday objects such as a child's rattle or a tradesperson's weights show how many ordinary people really lived. Returning to a house first dug more than a century ago, and exploring servants' quarters, helps build a fuller and fairer account of Roman society, including those who settled far from where they were born. Pompeii is still giving up its secrets, which is rather good news for anyone curious about the past.
