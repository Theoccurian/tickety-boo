Scientists in Brazil have pinpointed enzymes and gut microbes that seem to influence both methane emissions and feed efficiency in Nelore cattle, the country's most widely raised beef breed. The team at the Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation (Embrapa) hopes the work will lead to strategies that trim a powerful greenhouse gas while helping animals make better use of their food.

Luciana Regitano, a researcher at Embrapa Pecuária Sudeste in São Carlos, São Paulo, shared some of the findings on Wednesday 28 October at FAPESP Week Netherlands. Her group combines several molecular techniques to examine DNA, RNA, proteins and other chemicals in the cattle and their gut microbiota all at once.

Methane is a by-product of digestion. Microorganisms in the rumen, the largest chamber of a cow's stomach, break down plant fibre and release the gas as they go. The process also swallows between 2% and 12% of the energy the animal would otherwise draw from its feed.

By mapping RNA molecules in the rumen, the researchers found enzymes linked to better feed use and lower methane production. These are potential targets for new feed additives. Commercial additives already exist, but Embrapa's data reveal fresh metabolic pathways that could allow more precise inhibitors.

There is a catch. Methane formation mops up spare hydrogen left by fermentation, so simply blocking it can leave hydrogen to build up, turning the stomach more acidic and hampering digestion. The team therefore wants to steer that hydrogen towards useful routes, such as making propionate, a fatty acid that gives the animal energy.

Probiotics and prebiotics are another avenue, aimed at helping bacteria associated with low emissions settle in the rumen for good. The researchers are also testing an idea, so far based on computer simulations, that cattle use tiny molecules called microRNAs to keep their own gut microbes in check.

A study with the Brazilian Association of Zebu Breeders followed Nelore cattle from pasture to feedlot. Microbes found in a calf's stomach on pasture were strongly linked to feed efficiency and weight gain months later.

Challenges remain. Choosing cattle directly for their microbiota does not yet pay its way, because sequencing is expensive next to the gains. Meanwhile, around 40% of what the team identified is absent from international databases, and roughly 48% of the species found may be unknown to science. Mapping all that is no mean feat, so Regitano has teamed up with bioinformatics and artificial intelligence specialists to predict gene functions faster, before isolating the microbes for laboratory tests.

## Why it matters

Cattle rearing is among the major sources of greenhouse gases, and methane from digestion is part of that picture. A clearer understanding of the microbes behind it could help researchers design more targeted ways to cut emissions. The findings are still at the research stage, and any practical products would need further testing, but a better map of the rumen is a welcome step.
