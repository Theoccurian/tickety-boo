An orphaned walrus calf found on a remote Alaskan beach now lives at SeaWorld Orlando, where he has started to meet his new herd. It follows a rescue that took several days and a long stretch of rehabilitation.

On 11 September, college student Violet Anniskett was working on a beach in northwestern Alaska when she spotted a living calf lying between two dead adult walruses. "I was in shock," she said. She was there on an internship with Tony Fischbach, a wildlife biologist with the US Geological Survey, and the pair were hunting for fresh carcasses to sample for algal toxins, to help establish normal levels in Pacific walruses.

They asked the US Fish and Wildlife Service, and the local Inupiaq community to which Anniskett belongs, for approval to care for the male calf, who was around five months old. They named him Annautaq, after her father. Anniskett said asking the community first mattered a great deal.

Reaching help was no mean feat. The Alaska SeaLife Center, the state's only permanent marine mammal rehabilitation facility, lies more than 800 miles away in Seward. Storms kept the 185-pound calf grounded for days, so the researchers looked after him themselves while gulls and two polar bears were around.

They kept him in an abandoned building for almost four days. Having no formula, they gave him honey mixed with water. He snuggled into Fischbach's sleeping bag and struggled to nod off unless one of them was nearby.

With help from locals and fellow researchers, the calf got to the airport, and he arrived at the SeaLife Center on 14 September. He was dehydrated but in good shape overall, and minor wounds were treated with medication.

Walrus calves normally stay with their mothers for one to two years, so carers stepped in as stand-in parents, offering constant contact, company, feeding and veterinary care. Annautaq did well. However, the US Fish and Wildlife Service judged that he could not go back to the wild, because he would grow too accustomed to people.

Experts picked SeaWorld Orlando as his lasting home, and the park says he will help to represent his species. He now weighs about 250 pounds and takes up to six bottles a day. Having completed quarantine, he has been introduced to Uki, a two-year-old walrus also rescued in Alaska, and the two touched noses through a barrier.

## Why it matters

Walrus calves rely on their mothers for milk and close contact for a long time, which is why a lone youngster needs such intensive care. This plucky calf's story is a reminder of how much effort goes into looking after one animal. It also shows that when a return to the wild is ruled out, a settled life among other walruses can still be arranged.
