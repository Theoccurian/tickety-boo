Scientists in California have pulled apart two effects of psilocybin, the psychedelic compound in "magic mushrooms", in mice. One is its ability to strengthen connections between brain cells. The other is the hallucinogenic trip. The study, from the University of California, Santa Cruz, appears in Nature Neuroscience.

Psilocybin is being explored as a possible treatment for depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Ideally, researchers would like to keep any benefit to the brain while leaving out the trip that normally comes with it.

The team gave psilocybin to groups of mice and examined their dendritic spines, the tiny contact points that let nerve cells talk to one another. In less than a day, the compound had enlarged these spines and hurried along their development. The trip-like experience was measured indirectly, by watching for head twitches.

The crucial step was altering the brain cells that respond to psilocybin. By genetically reconfiguring the "docking stations" on those cells, the researchers kept the spine changes but lost the twitches. That pointed to two distinct pathways: one for plasticity, which is the brain's ability to adapt and rewire itself, and one for hallucinations.

The brain's starting condition made a difference too. In mice that had been stressed to mimic depression, new spines formed at a higher rate and the loss of existing connections was halted. In unstressed mice, psilocybin sped up both the growth of new spines and the clearing away of old ones.

Molecular biologist Yi Zuo, one of the researchers, said: "I consider this work a major advance."

It is early days, and a sensible note of caution applies. The experiments involved only mice, so the findings will need to be translated to people. The team's next steps include identifying the specific brain cells behind the hallucination responses and comparing spine production with changes in the animals' mood.

## Why it matters

Until now, it was unclear whether psilocybin's neuroplasticity benefits depended on its psychedelic effects. This work strongly suggests they do not, which is useful knowledge for anyone designing future studies. Zuo has suggested that therapies built on this idea could offer fast, lasting antidepressant benefits without hallucinogenic side effects, and might be safer and easier to offer to more patients. That remains a hope rather than a result, but it is a welcome step on the road.
