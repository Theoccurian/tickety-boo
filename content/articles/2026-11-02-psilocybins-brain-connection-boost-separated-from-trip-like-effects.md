Scientists in California have shown that the brain-rewiring benefits of psilocybin can be pulled apart from its hallucinogenic effects. It is an early but rather encouraging step towards possible treatments for depression and PTSD that would not involve a psychedelic trip.

The team, from the University of California, Santa Cruz, reported in Nature Neuroscience that two separate pathways appear to be at work. One drives plasticity, meaning the ability of brain cells to adapt and rewire themselves. The other produces hallucinations. By genetically altering the docking points on cells that psilocybin latches on to, the researchers kept the first response while shutting off the second.

They concentrated on dendritic spines, the tiny junctions that let nerve cells talk to one another. Within a day, psilocybin had enlarged these spines and hastened their development. That gain held up even when the hallucinogenic response had been switched off.

The effect also varied with the state of the brain. Under stress designed to approximate depression, psilocybin produced a larger surge of new spines and halted the loss of existing connections. Without that stress, it quickened both the growth of fresh spines and the clearing of older ones.

Molecular biologist Yi Zuo called the work "a major advance". She said the results cast doubt on the common assumption that psilocybin simply adds more connections everywhere, and suggested its influence depends on what condition the brain is already in.

The researchers are candid about the stage this has reached. This is early laboratory research that has not yet been tested in people, so the findings must be translated into human studies before anyone can say whether they hold true for patients.

The next steps are to identify the particular brain cells behind the hallucinogenic response, and to compare spine formation with changes in mood, so that the whole picture becomes clearer.

## Why it matters

Until now, it was unclear whether psilocybin's capacity to boost neuroplasticity relied on its psychedelic qualities. This work strongly suggests it does not, which is valuable knowledge for anyone designing future therapies. A treatment that delivered fast, lasting antidepressant benefits without hallucinations might prove safer and easier to offer to a wider range of patients. There is a long road ahead, but the direction of travel is promising.
