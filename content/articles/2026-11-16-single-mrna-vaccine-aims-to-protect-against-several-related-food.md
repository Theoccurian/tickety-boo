Researchers at DTU have developed an mRNA vaccine that could one day protect people against several food allergies at once. In early laboratory studies, it prompted the body to make protective antibodies against a group of related allergens, including those found in peaches and certain nuts. The work is preclinical research and has not yet been tested in people.

The vaccine contains a synthetic allergen that shares features with a number of widespread natural ones. Its job is to reprogramme the immune system, so that when it meets the real thing it already has antibodies ready to recognise and block the allergen before a reaction begins.

"We use the mRNA as a Trojan horse," says Associate Professor Esperanza Rivera de Torre of DTU Bioengineering. Rather than feeding the body the allergen to build up tolerance, the vaccine gives it instructions on how to deal with the allergen properly.

The results, published in Nature Communications, are rather encouraging. The vaccine produced a strong antibody response with broad coverage across the targeted allergens, and virtually no side effects were seen. In tests on blood samples from allergic patients, the antibodies reduced allergic reactions.

There is still work to do. At the doses and schedule tested so far, the vaccine did not give full protection against anaphylaxis, the most severe type of reaction. The team plans larger studies to establish how often, and in what amounts, it should be given. They are also refining the lipid bubbles that carry the allergen, and are developing a similar approach for allergens found in apples, carrots and hazelnuts.

The researchers describe the work as a proof of concept. Their overarching aim is to gather the data needed for approval to begin trials in humans.

## Why it matters

More than 220 million people worldwide have food allergies, according to U.N. figures. The only treatment that can potentially cure them involves years of injections or tablets, and many people understandably choose to avoid the foods instead. A vaccine that covers several related allergens in one go could, if it proves safe and effective in people, offer a simpler route to protection. That is still some way off, but it is a promising start.
