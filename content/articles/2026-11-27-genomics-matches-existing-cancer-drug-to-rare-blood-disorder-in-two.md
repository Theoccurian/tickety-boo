Two adult sisters from the West Midlands have become the first patients to receive dasatinib, a drug already used against blood cancer, for a very rare blood disorder. Genomic sequencing allowed doctors and scientists to pinpoint what the medicine should aim at. The findings appear in Blood Journal.

The condition is SRC-related thrombocytopenia. It causes a gene to keep producing a protein that plays a part in making and activating platelets, so patients end up with unusually low platelet levels. Many also develop an enlarged spleen, immune difficulties and scarring in the bone marrow, where blood cells are made.

Until now, care has largely centred on transfusions to make up the shortfall, which means regular journeys to hospital.

A team of doctors and cardiovascular scientists in Birmingham used genetic testing to understand how the disorder works. They found that dasatinib can quieten the SRC protein, allowing platelet production to look more typical by reversing damage to the bone marrow.

Manjit Kaur began taking the drug about three years ago, after her sister responded well to it. Getting the dose right took some work, because she also has Crohn's disease, but she now takes an amount she can manage. Her progress is checked through hospital blood tests four times a year.

The results have been striking. Her blood counts have improved, she is less tired and she no longer needs transfusions. She was diagnosed in 1989 and spent much of her childhood in and out of hospital, missing lessons along the way. She went on to gain qualifications and has worked for 31 years as an IT administrator at a GP surgery, all the while keeping up her love of travel.

Ms Kaur hopes her story will encourage others to consider clinical trials or treatments they are offered. "Give it a go," she says.

Professor Neil Morgan of the University of Birmingham, who led the study, praised the close working between the university and the hospital through Birmingham Health Partners and Cancer Research UK. Dr Manoj Raghavan, the consultant haematologist who treated the sisters, said the insight into how bone marrow functions could be taken forward for other blood diseases. It is rather good news for a family that has lived with the condition for decades.

## Why it matters

Many rare diseases and conditions currently have no treatment. This work shows that genomics can reveal how medicines already in use might help, and the researchers say it points the way for other conditions. For these sisters, it has already meant a real change to everyday life, with an end to regular transfusions in Ms Kaur's case.
