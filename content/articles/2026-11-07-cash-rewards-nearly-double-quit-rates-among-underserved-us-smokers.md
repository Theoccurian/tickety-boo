Paying people a reward for giving up cigarettes has almost doubled the number who succeed, according to a large American trial published in JAMA. The participants were adults from medically underserved communities, and all were being screened for lung cancer.

The study recruited more than 3,200 people through five medical centres, among them Penn Medicine, Geisinger and Kaiser Permanente Southern California, as well as Henry Ford Health. Many volunteers came from groups that often find good care hard to reach, including people on low incomes, rural residents, and Black and Hispanic adults.

Researchers tested four different ways of helping people quit. Six months on, 8.8% of those offered free stop-smoking medication and the chance to earn up to $600 had stopped. Among those given usual care, the figure was 4.3%. Blood or urine tests confirmed who had really quit, so nobody was simply taking it on trust.

Christine Neslund-Dudas, who worked on the study at Henry Ford Health, pointed out that many participants had smoked for years and probably tried to stop before. She called the results encouraging because they offer "another tool to consider" for people facing extra obstacles.

The findings also underline how useful a screening appointment can be. Mike Simoff, a pulmonologist at Henry Ford Health, said screening can spot cancer sooner and open a conversation about one of the biggest risk factors. The organisation already builds tobacco treatment into its central screening programme. Its free telephone service uses motivational interviewing, which helps people explore their reasons for smoking and plan changes, and medication can ease cravings and withdrawal.

Amanda Holm, who supports the service, suggested that a tangible reward might build confidence, and could mean a good deal to someone with little money to spare.

Rather good news, then, though practical questions remain. Nobody yet knows who would pay for incentives in routine care, how much might be saved on long-term health costs, or how such schemes would reach people with limited resources.

## Why it matters

Smoking is the leading cause of lung cancer and also raises the risk of other cancers and cardiovascular disease, yet people with the fewest resources often find it hardest to stop. Doubling the success rate, with results checked by laboratory tests, is a cracking result for a group that is easily overlooked. If health systems can sort out the funding, pairing screening with treatment and rewards could help narrow a stubborn health gap.
