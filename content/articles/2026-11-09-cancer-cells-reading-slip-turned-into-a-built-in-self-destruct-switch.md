Scientists have found a way to turn a quirk of cancer cells into a trigger for their own destruction. In a study published in Nature, researchers built a synthetic genetic circuit that switches on only in cancer cells and prompts them to burst, while healthy cells carry on undisturbed. The work is early laboratory research and has not yet been tested in people.

The quirk in question is called ribosomal frameshifting. Ribosomes, the cell's protein-making machinery, read genetic instructions in blocks of three letters. Occasionally one slips and shifts its reading position, an event thought to be extremely rare, roughly once in every 100,000 translation events.

The team screened a range of cancer cell lines, including melanoma, liver, colorectal and ovarian cancer. They found that certain pairs of codons, such as AGAACG and ACGAGA, regularly make ribosomes in cancer cells slip back by a single letter. Healthy cells read the very same sequences in the normal way.

The researchers traced the slippage to heightened phosphorylation of a ribosomal protein called S6. This is driven by the mTOR and MAPK growth pathways, which are often overactive in cancer.

That difference gave them something to work with. Their circuit, named FAST, places the pore-forming fragment of a protein called gasdermin D just behind a slip-prone sequence, in the shifted reading frame. Only when a ribosome slips is the fragment made. It then punches holes in the cell membrane, causing an inflammatory form of cell death known as pyroptosis.

In early laboratory studies, FAST delivered intravenously caused substantial tumour regression in colorectal and pancreatic cancer models. It also curbed spread to the liver and lungs, boosted immune activity inside tumours, and worked better alongside immune checkpoint inhibitors. Tests found no inflammation or toxicity in the healthy tissue of major organs.

It is a rather neat trick: rather than fighting the cancer cell's machinery, the approach borrows its mistakes.

## Why it matters

A persistent difficulty in cancer treatment is making a powerful therapy act on tumour cells without harming healthy tissue, which is a major cause of side effects. This work points to a new kind of selectivity, based on how cancer cells read their own genetic instructions. Much more research, including human trials, will be needed, but the findings suggest frameshift-triggered gene circuits could one day become a way to target cancer cells more precisely.
