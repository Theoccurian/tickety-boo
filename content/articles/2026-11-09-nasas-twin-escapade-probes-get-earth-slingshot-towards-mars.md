NASA's two ESCAPADE spacecraft have each swung past Earth and fired their engines, combining the planet's gravity with a long burn to fling themselves towards Mars. They are now on target to arrive in September 2027. NASA says it has never before used a powered Earth flyby to send a craft to the Red Planet, and here it managed the feat twice in two days.

The first spacecraft made its closest approach at 5 a.m. EST on 7 November, around 1,000 miles above the surface. Its engine burned for about nine minutes, the firing that commits it to the Mars route. The second craft did the same at 6 a.m. EST on 9 November, passing roughly 650 miles up.

It has been a rather unusual road to this point. The pair left Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida on 13 November 2025, with the Sun sitting between Earth and Mars. Instead of heading straight out, they flew a kidney-bean-shaped path around Lagrange point 2, a gravitational balancing spot roughly a million miles away on the far side of Earth from the Sun, before doubling back for their slingshot.

Lead mission designer Jeff Parker of Advanced Space said the technique lets a mission set off at almost any time. Mars-bound craft sent directly must wait for a launch window, which comes around only every two years.

Ahead lies a 10-month cruise with a few course corrections. Each spacecraft will then burn its engine for about 11 minutes to enter Mars orbit, on 7 and 9 September 2027. Science operations are expected to begin in June 2028.

The plucky pair will study the solar wind, a stream of material from the Sun moving at about a million miles per hour, and how it interacts with the Martian environment to drive atmospheric loss. Six months into the work, one craft will move farther out to watch the incoming solar wind while the other stays close to see how Mars responds. That phase is scheduled to last five months.

The mission is led by the University of California, Berkeley's Space Sciences Laboratory. Partners include Rocket Lab, NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University, Advanced Space and Blue Origin. It forms part of NASA's SIMPLEx programme, which backs innovative missions on smaller budgets.

## Why it matters

Mars missions have usually been tied to launch windows that open only every couple of years, so a method that loosens that constraint could make future journeys easier to plan. ESCAPADE's two orbiters will also give scientists their first coordinated observations of the Martian environment from two places at once. That should improve understanding of how the solar wind affects the planet, which NASA says could help it prepare for future human exploration.
