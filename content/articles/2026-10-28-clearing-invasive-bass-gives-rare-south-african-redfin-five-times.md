A critically endangered South African fish now has five times as much river to call home, after conservationists cleared an invasive predator from the Breekkrans River, about two hours north of Cape Town.

The doring fiery redfin (Pseudobarbus doring) had been squeezed into a 2-kilometre stretch that the invaders had never reached. Thanks to a restoration project, its habitat now runs to 10 kilometres, which is a cracking result for a species with so little room to spare.

Spotted bass were introduced to the river system in the late 1930s, mainly to please anglers. Martine Jordaan, a faunal ecologist at Cape Nature, says the qualities that make bass good sport also make them a dreadful invader: they hunt small fish relentlessly, and most native species are 12 centimetres or smaller. The bass spread as far as the habitat allowed, stopping only at natural barriers such as waterfalls, so native fish survived mainly in streams the bass could not enter.

The Breekkrans holds the largest remaining population of the redfin, probably a few hundred adults, according to Jeremy Shelton, a freshwater conservation ecologist at the Freshwater Research Centre. The species survives in just one other river, and only in very low numbers.

The Breekkrans River Restoration Project is led by the centre, working alongside Cape Nature and the Fynbos Fish Trust. Since 2023, teams have gone after the bass every Southern Hemisphere summer, using nets, spear guns and electrofishers along 8 kilometres of the upper river. A fish barrier built downstream stops the bass from swimming back in.

The benefits have reached well beyond one species. “All native fish showed recovery,” Jordaan said, adding that every species had widened its range as a direct result of removing the predatory bass.

Shelton said the work shows that invasive bass can be completely and permanently removed from entire stretches of river by mechanical methods, allowing threatened native fish to recover. He described it as a major breakthrough for freshwater conservation in South Africa, one that opens the door to restoring other invaded rivers.

## Why it matters

When a species is confined to a tiny stretch of water, a single threat can wipe it out, so every extra kilometre of safe habitat improves its odds. Invasive predators are a well-known danger to freshwater fish worldwide, and removing them is often thought to be very hard once they are established. Showing that it can be done, and that native fish bounce back afterwards, offers a practical template for other rivers.
