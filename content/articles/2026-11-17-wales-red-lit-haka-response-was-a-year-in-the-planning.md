The red light and smoke that enveloped Wales' players as New Zealand performed their haka at the Principality Stadium took a full year to plan, according to one of the people behind it.

Alex Luff, the stadium's head of event sales and operations, said the idea began after the previous November's fixture between the two sides, when the haka was staged in a fresh, theatrical way. That display was all about the haka itself. This time, the organisers wanted the spotlight on how Wales would answer the challenge.

On Saturday, the home players stood in a red glow and stared across at their opponents. The scene set the tone for a defiant performance and a historic win.

Luff explained that the spectacle depended on many moving parts. The band, the choir and the pyrotechnics all had to be layered together with painstaking care. "It's like a military operation," he said, with the timing worked out to the second.

The trickiest stretch came straight after the national anthems. Organisers had just ninety seconds to get a choir of 150, a military band of 50 and a display team of 50 off the pitch. Only then could the production equipment be brought on to prepare for the haka.

The effort was matched by what followed on the field. Steve Tandy's side beat New Zealand for the first time in 73 years, bringing to an end a run of 34 defeats against the All Blacks that dated back to 1953. Joyous celebrations broke out in Cardiff and well beyond it, which made for a cracking result all round.

Luff said the response to the display had been extraordinary, and he hoped people would remember it for as long as they remember the scoreline. In his view, it showed what Welsh rugby and the Principality Stadium are all about.

## Why it matters

Big sporting occasions depend on far more than the players, and this one is a reminder of how much unseen preparation goes into creating an atmosphere. For a team ending a losing run that had lasted generations, a carefully built moment of defiance gave supporters something shared to savour, and a memory that reaches well beyond the final whistle.
