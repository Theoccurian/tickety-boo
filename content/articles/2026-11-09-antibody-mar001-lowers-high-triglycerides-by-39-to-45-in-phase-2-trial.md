An experimental antibody treatment has produced large drops in triglyceride levels among people whose readings were too high, according to a phase 2b trial led by researchers at the Mass General Brigham Heart and Vascular Institute. The therapy, known as MAR001, cut fasting triglycerides significantly by the 12-week mark. The results appear in the New England Journal of Medicine and were shared at the same time with delegates at the American Heart Association's annual meeting.

In total, 235 participants with raised triglycerides took part. Each was randomly allocated either a placebo or one of three doses of MAR001 (300, 450 or 900 mg), injected under the skin once every four weeks for 24 weeks. Treatment was delivered at 48 centres in the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, and the study was designed by the TIMI Study Group together with the sponsor, Marea Therapeutics.

Compared with placebo, fasting triglycerides at week 12 were lower by between 39.1% and 45.5%, depending on the dose. Adverse effects were not more frequent among those given MAR001 than among those given the placebo.

The drug takes a fresh approach. Several types of medicine are already known to reduce triglycerides, but MAR001 targets a protein called ANGPTL4, which naturally restrains an enzyme, lipoprotein lipase, that helps break triglycerides down.

Lead author Michelle O'Donoghue, a cardiologist at the institute, pointed out that many patients still have high triglycerides and cardiovascular risk despite the progress made with cholesterol and fat-lowering drugs. She said the team also saw bigger falls in people who began with higher triglyceride levels, and that a dedicated follow-up study is now looking at exactly that.

That next stage will test MAR001 in people whose triglycerides are severely raised. The researchers stress that questions remain: they still need to learn how the treatment performs over longer periods, and whether lowering triglycerides this way actually reduces the chance of pancreatitis and other complications.

## Why it matters

Triglycerides are a type of fat in the blood, and persistently high levels are linked with heart disease and, when very high, with inflammation of the pancreas. Some patients still struggle with raised readings even on current treatments, so a medicine that works through a different route could be a welcome boost. It is still early days, and larger, longer trials will have to show whether the lower numbers translate into better health.
